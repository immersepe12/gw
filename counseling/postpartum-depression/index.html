Postpartum Depression Counseling in Modesto

Postpartum depression is a common, treatable condition that can start during pregnancy or in the year after birth. At Generations Wellness, licensed counselors offer talk therapy for new parents in person in downtown Modesto or by telehealth, so you can get support without a long drive.

Understanding PPD

Baby Blues vs. Postpartum Depression

Many new mothers feel weepy, moody or overwhelmed in the first days after birth. This is often called the baby blues. The National Institute of Mental Health notes the baby blues are mild and usually go away on their own within about two weeks.

Postpartum depression (PPD) is different. It is more intense, lasts longer and makes it hard to care for yourself or your baby. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) notes that perinatal depression is one of the most common complications of pregnancy and that it can begin during pregnancy, not only after birth.

  • Baby blues: mood swings and tearfulness that come and go, start within a few days, and fade within about two weeks.
  • Postpartum depression: sadness, emptiness or anxiety most of the day, lasting more than two weeks, that gets in the way of daily life.

PPD is not caused by anything you did. Hormone changes, sleep loss, stress, past depression and a lack of support can all play a part. Partners and adoptive parents can have depression after a new baby arrives too.

Signs to Watch

Common PPD Symptoms

  • Persistent sadness, hopelessness or feeling empty.
  • Constant worry or racing, scary thoughts about the baby.
  • Feeling disconnected from your baby, or guilty that you do not feel what you expected.
  • Not sleeping even when the baby sleeps, or sleeping all the time.
  • Irritability or anger that feels out of character.
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby. Call or text 988 now.

Many parents hide these feelings because they feel ashamed. Telling your OB, midwife, pediatrician or primary care doctor is a strong first step. ACOG recommends screening for depression during pregnancy and after birth, and your baby's pediatrician may ask about your mood at well-baby visits.

Free support lines

  • National Maternal Mental Health Hotline: call or text 1-833-852-6262, free and confidential, 24 hours a day, in English and Spanish.
  • Postpartum Support International HelpLine: 1-800-944-4773, for information and connection to local support.

Emergency

Postpartum Psychosis Needs Emergency Care

Postpartum psychosis is rare but serious. It usually starts suddenly in the first weeks after birth. Signs include seeing or hearing things that are not there, believing things that are not true, extreme confusion, paranoia, not sleeping for days, or rapid mood swings.

Postpartum psychosis is a medical emergency. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room right away. Do not leave the person alone with the baby. Counseling is not the right first step for psychosis; emergency medical care is.

Approaches

How Counseling Helps With Postpartum Depression

The NIMH lists psychotherapy as an effective treatment for perinatal depression, alone or combined with medicine. Two approaches are commonly used:

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

CBT helps you notice harsh thoughts like "I'm a bad mother" or "Everyone else is handling this," test them, and replace them with kinder, more accurate ones. It also helps you plan small, doable steps back toward rest, movement and connection.

Interpersonal therapy (IPT)

IPT focuses on relationships and role changes. Becoming a parent changes your identity, your partnership, your work and your friendships. IPT helps you work through those shifts and ask for the support you need.

Practical support

Sessions often cover protecting sleep in shifts, accepting help, managing intrusive thoughts, and talking with your partner. You are welcome to bring your baby to in-person sessions, or use telehealth during nap time.

Medication questions

Counselors do not prescribe. Medication, including whether it is compatible with breastfeeding, is a decision for you and your OB or physician. Talk with them about options; counseling can continue alongside any medical treatment.

Questions

Postpartum Depression: Frequently Asked Questions

How long do the baby blues last?

The baby blues usually begin a few days after birth and fade within about two weeks without treatment. If sadness, worry or numbness lasts longer than two weeks, gets stronger, or makes it hard to care for yourself or your baby, talk with your OB or doctor and consider counseling. It may be postpartum depression.

Can postpartum depression start months after birth?

Yes. Perinatal depression can begin during pregnancy or any time in the first year after birth. Some parents notice it when they go back to work, when breastfeeding ends, or when sleep loss builds up. Late onset does not make it less real, and support is still worth seeking whenever symptoms appear.

Will someone take my baby away if I say I am struggling?

This is a very common fear. Having depression, anxiety or upsetting intrusive thoughts is not a reason in itself for a child to be removed. Counselors and doctors want to help you get well and care for your baby. Your counselor will explain privacy and its limits at your first session.

Can dads and partners get postpartum depression?

Yes. Fathers, partners and adoptive parents can experience depression and anxiety after a new baby arrives. Sleep loss, stress, money worries and relationship changes affect the whole family. Our counselors see adults of any gender, and Postpartum Support International also offers resources for dads and partners.

Can I bring my baby to counseling?

Please ask when you call so we can plan the visit. Many new parents prefer telehealth because they can join from home during a nap or with the baby nearby. Video sessions use the same approach as in-person counseling and are available as long as you are in California during the session.
