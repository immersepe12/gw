Understanding Trauma

Normal Reactions After a Traumatic Event

Trauma can follow a car crash, an assault, abuse, combat, a fire, a sudden death, a medical emergency or years of living with danger. It can also come from seeing something happen to someone else.

The National Institute of Mental Health explains that it is natural to feel afraid during and after a traumatic event. In the first days and weeks, many people have trouble sleeping, replay what happened, feel jumpy, or feel numb. For most people these reactions ease over time, especially with support from people they trust.

When reactions do not fade, or they get worse, it may be post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or another trauma-related problem such as depression, anxiety or heavier drinking. That is not a personal failing. It means the brain's alarm system is stuck on, and there are well-studied ways to help.

Signs of PTSD

What PTSD Can Look Like

According to the NIMH and the VA National Center for PTSD, symptoms usually fall into four groups and last more than a month:

  • Reliving it. Unwanted memories, nightmares or flashbacks that feel like it is happening again.
  • Avoiding reminders. Staying away from places, people, sounds or conversations linked to the event.
  • Changes in thoughts and mood. Guilt, shame, blaming yourself, feeling cut off from others, or trouble feeling positive emotions.
  • Feeling on guard. Being easily startled, irritable, unable to sleep or concentrate, or always scanning for danger.

Symptoms can start soon after the event or show up months or years later. Only a licensed clinician can make a diagnosis, and you do not need one to start counseling.

Approaches

Evidence-Based Trauma Therapies

The VA National Center for PTSD describes several trauma-focused talk therapies with strong research support. You may hear these names:

  • Trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): learning how trauma changed your thoughts and reactions, and practicing new ways to respond.
  • Cognitive processing therapy (CPT): examining stuck beliefs after trauma, such as "It was my fault" or "Nowhere is safe," and building more balanced ones.
  • Prolonged exposure (PE): gradually and safely approaching memories and situations you have been avoiding, so they lose some of their power.
  • Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR): focusing on a memory while doing guided side-to-side eye movements or other rhythmic tasks.

Each of these takes specific training. Approaches vary between counselors, so ask which approaches your counselor uses and what their experience with trauma is. That is a fair and common question.

Safety and pacing come first

Good trauma therapy does not push you to tell every detail on day one. Early sessions often focus on building trust, understanding your reactions, and learning grounding and calming skills you can use when memories hit. You stay in charge of the pace.

Medication questions

Some people with PTSD also benefit from medicine. Counselors do not prescribe. If you want to discuss medication, talk with a physician such as your primary care doctor.

Young People

Trauma in Teens

Teens can develop PTSD too. It may look like anger, risky behavior, falling grades, pulling away from friends, trouble sleeping, or acting younger than their age. Some teens act as if nothing happened. Trauma-focused CBT has been studied in children and teens and usually involves a parent or caregiver.

Services for younger clients depend on clinician availability, so please call to ask. Our guide to counseling for teens and children explains warning signs and California consent rules. If a child is being harmed, contact law enforcement or child protective services.

Questions

Trauma Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to talk about what happened?

Not right away, and never before you are ready. Early sessions usually focus on safety, trust and coping skills. Some trauma-focused therapies do involve working with memories, but your counselor explains the plan first and you agree on the pace. You can pause or change direction at any time.

Can PTSD show up years after the event?

Yes. The NIMH notes symptoms usually begin within three months but can start much later. A new stress, an anniversary or a reminder can bring old reactions to the surface. It is never too late to seek help, and counseling can still be useful many years after a traumatic event.

Do your counselors offer EMDR or CPT?

Training varies by counselor, so please ask which approaches your counselor uses when you call or at your first session. If you are looking for one specific therapy, say so on the screening call. We can tell you honestly whether it fits our current availability or whether another provider may suit you better.

Is trauma counseling available by telehealth?

Yes, by secure video while you are in California. Many people feel safer starting from home. Your counselor will talk with you about privacy, a quiet space and a plan for what to do if you feel overwhelmed during or after a video session, so you feel supported either way.

How long does trauma therapy take?

It varies. Some structured trauma therapies are designed to run a set number of weekly sessions, while other people work longer on several experiences or related concerns. Your counselor will discuss a plan and review progress with you regularly. Results differ from person to person, and no one can promise a timeline.
