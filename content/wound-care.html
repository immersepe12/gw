Know the Signs

When a Wound Needs More Than Home Care

Most cuts and scrapes heal on their own in a couple of weeks. Some wounds don't. A wound that has not healed after about four to six weeks is often called a chronic or non-healing wound. Something is usually getting in the way: poor blood flow, high blood sugar, pressure, swelling, infection or a health condition that slows repair.

See a clinician soon if:

  • A wound shows no clear improvement after two weeks, or is not healed in four to six weeks
  • You have diabetes and any sore, blister or cut on your foot
  • A surgical incision opens, drains or becomes more painful
  • A wound keeps coming back in the same place
  • Skin over a bony area stays red or breaks down in someone who is bedbound or uses a wheelchair

Getting care early matters. Wounds caught sooner are often easier to manage than wounds that have been open for months.

Our Approach

How Outpatient Wound Care Works

Good wound care treats the cause, not just the hole in the skin. At our practice, a licensed clinician assesses your wound before any treatment plan is made, and a physician leads your care. A plan may include:

  • Assessment: measuring the wound, checking for infection, and looking at blood flow, sensation, swelling and blood sugar
  • Cleaning and dressings chosen for the wound type, with clear instructions for care at home
  • Treating the cause: compression for venous ulcers, pressure relief for pressure injuries, offloading for diabetic foot ulcers, blood sugar control
  • Referral to vascular, surgical or other specialists when a wound needs care we don't provide
  • Regular follow-up to measure progress and change the plan if healing stalls

Because we are a multi-service practice, your wound plan can be shared with other services, such as physical therapy for mobility or pain management, so everyone works from the same plan.

Root Causes

What Slows Wound Healing

Healing needs a steady supply of blood, oxygen, protein and a clean wound bed. Many everyday health factors interfere with that. Finding and working on these is often the most important part of a wound plan:

  • Diabetes, especially when blood sugar runs high, which slows the body's repair and its ability to fight infection
  • Poor circulation, from narrowed arteries (peripheral artery disease) or weak leg veins
  • Swelling in the legs, which stretches skin and slows healing
  • Pressure or friction on the same spot, from shoes, beds or wheelchairs
  • Smoking and vaping, which narrow blood vessels and reduce oxygen to tissue
  • Poor nutrition, especially too little protein
  • Infection in the wound or the bone beneath it
  • Some medicines, such as steroids or certain cancer treatments, and past radiation to the area

Your care plan may involve your primary care doctor or other specialists to work on these, and we are glad to coordinate with them with your permission.

Between Visits

Caring for a Wound at Home

What happens at home between visits counts as much as what happens in the clinic. Your clinician will give you instructions for your specific wound. In general:

  • Wash your hands before and after touching the wound or dressing.
  • Change dressings as directed, not more or less often, and use the supplies you were given.
  • Don't soak a chronic wound or put home remedies on it unless your clinician says to.
  • Follow your offloading, compression or repositioning plan every day.
  • Eat regular meals with protein, and keep blood sugar in your target range if you have diabetes.
  • Check the wound each day and call us if it gets larger, more painful, more swollen or starts to smell.

Family members and caregivers are welcome at visits, and we can show them how to help.

Hyperbaric Oxygen

Where Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Fits

Our practice also has hard-shell monoplace hyperbaric chambers. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) is used as an adjunct, an add-on to good wound care, for certain FDA-cleared indications, including selected diabetic foot ulcers, radiation tissue injury, compromised skin grafts and flaps, and chronic refractory osteomyelitis.

HBOT is not right for every wound. Venous leg ulcers and pressure injuries are not HBOT indications; they are managed with compression, pressure relief and standard wound care. Your physician determines whether HBOT is appropriate after an assessment. Read when wounds need hyperbaric oxygen or visit our hyperbaric oxygen therapy page.

Questions

Wound Care in Modesto: Frequently Asked Questions

When should I see a wound care clinic?

When a wound hasn't clearly improved in two weeks or healed in four to six, or right away for any foot wound if you have diabetes. A wound that keeps coming back in the same place, or a surgical incision that opens or drains, also needs a clinician's look.

Do I need a referral?

Call us and we will explain how to get started. We are glad to coordinate with your primary care doctor or surgeon with your permission. A licensed clinician assesses your wound before any treatment plan is made, and a physician leads your care.

Will I need hyperbaric oxygen therapy?

Most wounds don't need it. HBOT is used only for FDA-cleared indications, such as selected diabetic foot ulcers, and only when your physician decides it is appropriate. Good wound care treats the cause first, with cleaning, dressings and steps like offloading or compression.

How long does a chronic wound take to heal?

It varies widely, from weeks to months, depending on the cause, size, blood flow and your health. We measure progress at each visit and adjust the plan. Wounds caught sooner are often easier to manage than wounds that have been open for months.

Is wound care an emergency service?

No. We are an outpatient practice. Spreading redness with fever, black tissue or severe bleeding needs same-day or emergency care. We can refer you to vascular, surgical or other specialists when a wound needs care we don't provide. If you are unsure, seek care right away.
