Understanding

Autism and Communication

Autism is a lifelong developmental difference that affects how people communicate, interact and experience the world. Every autistic child is different. Some talk a lot, some use a few words, and some communicate mostly without speech. ASHA describes communication differences that are common in autistic children, including:

  • Starting to talk later, or using words in unexpected ways, such as repeating phrases from shows (echolalia), which is often a meaningful way to communicate.
  • Different ways of sharing attention, such as less pointing to show things.
  • Preferring to talk about favorite topics, or finding back-and-forth conversation tiring.
  • Understanding language very literally, or finding it hard to read tone and facial expressions.
  • Speaking in some situations but not others, especially when stressed or overwhelmed.

Communication is a two-way street. Good therapy helps your child express themselves and also helps the people around them understand your child's ways of communicating.

Diagnosis

Getting an Autism Diagnosis

Our speech-language pathologists do not diagnose autism. A diagnosis comes from professionals such as developmental-behavioral pediatricians, child psychologists, child psychiatrists or pediatric neurologists, often as part of a team.

The AAP recommends autism-specific screening at the 18- and 24-month well-child visits, along with general developmental screening. The CDC lists signs that may prompt a closer look, such as not responding to their name by 9 months, few gestures like pointing or waving by 12 months, or losing skills they had before. If you notice these, talk with your pediatrician. A speech evaluation can still be useful while you wait for a diagnostic appointment, because communication support does not need to wait for a label.

Speech delay on its own does not mean a child is autistic. Many late talkers are not. Read more about speech delay in toddlers.

Therapy Goals

What Speech Therapy Works On

Goals are set with you and, as much as possible, with your child. Depending on your child, a licensed speech-language pathologist may work on:

Social communication

Sharing attention, taking turns, starting and responding to interactions, and communicating for many reasons: asking, protesting, commenting, sharing joy. The aim is connection that feels natural to your child, not forced eye contact or scripted behavior.

Understanding language

Following directions, understanding questions, and making sense of words in daily routines, often with visual supports like picture schedules.

Expressive communication

Building words, phrases and sentences, or building skills with other ways of communicating. Echolalia is respected as communication and used as a bridge toward more flexible language.

Play

Play is where young children practice communication. Therapy builds on what your child already loves, whether that's trains, water, letters or music.

Self-advocacy

For older children, learning to say "I need a break," "too loud," or "I don't understand" can make school and family life easier.

Many autistic children also have sensory differences. Our occupational therapy for sensory processing can be coordinated with speech therapy under a shared care plan.

AAC

AAC: Pictures, Signs and Speech-Generating Devices

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) means any way of communicating besides speech. It can be:

  • Unaided: gestures and sign language.
  • Low-tech: picture cards, picture exchange, and communication boards.
  • High-tech: tablets or dedicated devices with apps that speak words aloud when your child taps a picture or word.

Will AAC stop my child from talking?

This is one of the most common worries we hear. ASHA reports that research does not show AAC stops children from developing speech, and some studies suggest it may support spoken language. AAC gives your child a way to communicate now, which can reduce frustration. Children don't need to "earn" AAC by showing certain skills first.

Supporting communication at home

Small changes in everyday routines can make a big difference: wait a little longer for your child to respond, offer choices with real objects or pictures, comment on what your child is doing instead of asking lots of questions, and celebrate every way your child communicates, including gestures, sounds and AAC.

Choosing a system involves your child's abilities, motor skills, interests and daily life. An SLP can help you try options and teach the whole family to model AAC by using it yourselves during everyday talk.

At Our Practice

Our Approach to Communication Support

  • Respect for your child. We support communication; we don't try to make your child less autistic.
  • Child-led, play-based sessions that follow your child's interests and sensory needs.
  • Parent coaching, because daily routines at home are where most communication happens.
  • Coordination, with your permission, with your pediatrician, school team, Regional Center service coordinator and our pediatric PT and OT team.
  • Honest expectations. Progress is real but looks different for every child, and no one can promise a particular result or timeline.

Be cautious of any program that promises to "recover" children from autism, or that sells unproven treatments. Speech therapy is about communication and quality of life, not about removing autism.

Regional Center, School and Private Speech Therapy

Families in Stanislaus County have several routes, and many use more than one:

  • Under 3: California Early Start, through Valley Mountain Regional Center, provides free early intervention for eligible infants and toddlers. You can refer your child yourself, with or without a diagnosis.

Questions

Autism and Speech Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Can speech therapy help a nonspeaking autistic child?

Yes. Therapy can build communication through AAC, signs and gestures, and support spoken language where possible. The goal is a reliable way for your child to communicate.

Does my child need an autism diagnosis to start speech therapy?

No. A speech evaluation looks at communication needs regardless of diagnosis. Some funding sources and programs do require a diagnosis, so ask about yours.

Will using AAC keep my child from talking?

Research summarized by ASHA does not show that AAC stops speech development, and some studies suggest it may support it.

Is echolalia a bad sign?

Not necessarily. Repeating words or phrases is often a meaningful way autistic children communicate, and SLPs can build on it toward more flexible language.

Can speech therapy make autism go away?

No. Autism is a lifelong difference. Speech therapy supports communication, understanding and participation, which can make daily life easier for your child and family.
