The Basics

What Is a Language Disorder?

Speech and language are not the same thing. Speech is the physical act of making sounds and words. Language is the system of words and rules we use to share ideas: vocabulary, grammar, putting sentences together, following directions and telling a story. A child can speak very clearly and still have a language disorder.

ASHA describes two main areas, and many children have difficulty with both:

  • Receptive language disorder: trouble understanding what others say or what they read. A child may struggle to follow directions, answer questions or understand stories.
  • Expressive language disorder: trouble putting thoughts into words. A child may use short or jumbled sentences, have trouble finding words, or leave out grammar like past tense endings.

A language disorder can happen on its own or alongside other conditions such as hearing loss, autism, ADHD, intellectual disability or brain injury. If your child is a toddler who is not yet talking much, our page on speech delay in toddlers is a better starting point.

DLD

Developmental Language Disorder (DLD)

Developmental language disorder, or DLD, is the term researchers and clinicians now use for a language disorder that persists into school age and is not explained by another condition like autism, hearing loss or intellectual disability. You may have heard older names such as "specific language impairment."

DLD is more common than most people think. A large U.S. study of kindergarten children by Tomblin and colleagues (1997) estimated that about 7% had specific language impairment, and a 2016 population study in England by Norbury and colleagues reported a similar figure for DLD, roughly 2 children in a typical classroom of 30. Most of those children had not been identified before the studies.

DLD is lifelong, but children can make real progress with support, and many learn strategies that help them in school and beyond. Children with DLD are just as smart as their peers; language is the specific area that is harder.

For Parents

Signs of a Language Disorder From Ages 3 to 12

Children vary, and one sign alone is not cause for alarm. Look for patterns that last several months. ASHA and NIDCD list signs like these:

Preschool (about 3 to 5)

  • Speaks in shorter or simpler sentences than other children the same age.
  • Has trouble following two-step directions without gestures.
  • Often answers questions with an unrelated reply or repeats the question.
  • Has a small vocabulary, or relies on words like "thing" and "that."
  • Leaves off word endings ("he walk") long after peers have stopped.

School age (about 6 to 12)

  • Struggles to retell a story or explain what happened in order.
  • Has trouble understanding questions in class, multistep directions or new vocabulary.
  • Finds it hard to find the right word, or talks around it.
  • Has trouble with reading comprehension, even if they can sound out words.
  • Has written work that is short, simple or disorganized.
  • Misses jokes, figurative language or social cues in conversation.

A hearing check is always a good first step, since even mild hearing loss can affect language.

Evaluation

How Speech-Language Pathologists Assess Language

A licensed speech-language pathologist (SLP) uses several sources of information, not just one test score:

  • A conversation with you about your child's history, home languages and concerns.
  • Standardized tests of vocabulary, grammar, understanding and storytelling.
  • A language sample, where the SLP listens to your child talk and tell a story.
  • Teacher input and school work samples, with your permission.
  • A recommendation for a hearing test if one has not been done recently.

Speaking two languages does not cause a language disorder. If your child is bilingual, the SLP will consider both languages; a true language disorder shows up in every language a child uses. Our pediatric speech and language evaluation page explains what to expect at the visit.

School

Language, Reading and School Support

Reading is built on language. Children with language disorders have a higher chance of reading and writing difficulties, especially with understanding what they read. Spotting the language problem early lets teachers and therapists support both at once.

If you think a language disorder is affecting your child's learning, you can ask your school district in writing for a free evaluation under IDEA. If your child qualifies, they can receive speech-language services through an Individualized Education Program (IEP). School services focus on what your child needs to succeed in the classroom. Some families add private therapy for more practice or for goals outside school. Our page on school vs private speech therapy compares the options.

At Our Practice

What Language Therapy Looks Like

  • Clear goals, like following classroom directions, using complete sentences or retelling a story in order.
  • Engaging activities such as games, books, story maps and real conversations, adjusted to your child's age.
  • Vocabulary and grammar practice tied to schoolwork when possible, so skills carry into the classroom.
  • Parent coaching with simple strategies for home, like expanding your child's sentences and talking through daily routines.
  • Coordination with teachers and school SLPs when you give permission, and with OT or counseling in our practice when needed.

Questions

Language Disorder in Children: Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a speech disorder and a language disorder?

A speech disorder affects how sounds and words are physically produced, such as unclear sounds or stuttering. A language disorder affects understanding and using words and sentences, such as vocabulary, grammar and following directions. A child can have one or both. A speech-language pathologist can evaluate both areas in the same visit.

Will my child outgrow a language disorder?

Some young children with language delays catch up, but language difficulties that continue into school age, such as DLD, usually do not simply go away. With therapy and school support, many children make real progress and learn strategies that help. Early evaluation gives your child the best chance to get the right support in time.

Does being bilingual cause a language disorder?

No. Learning two languages does not cause a language disorder, and you do not need to stop using your home language. Bilingual children may mix languages, which is normal. A true language disorder shows up in all of a child's languages, so an SLP will look at both when evaluating your child.

Can a language disorder affect reading?

Yes. Reading relies on vocabulary, grammar and understanding sentences, so children with language disorders are more likely to struggle with reading comprehension and writing. Some can sound out words well but still not understand what they read. Language therapy and school reading support can work together to help your child.

How do I get an IEP for a language disorder?

Write to your school principal or district special education office asking for a free evaluation for speech and language services under IDEA. The district must respond within set timelines. If your child qualifies, the team writes an IEP with goals and services. You can share private evaluation results with the school to inform their review.
