Telling the Difference

Typical Disfluency or Stuttering?

Almost every preschooler has bumpy speech sometimes. Language is growing faster than their speech can keep up. ASHA and the Stuttering Foundation describe differences that can help you notice which pattern you're hearing:

Common features of typical disfluency compared with stuttering (ASHA, Stuttering Foundation). Only an SLP can tell which applies to your child.
More typical disfluencyMore like stuttering
Repeating whole words or phrases: "I want, I want the cup"Repeating parts of words: "b-b-b-ball"
Fillers like "um" and "uh"Stretching sounds: "sssssun"
Relaxed, unaware of the bumpsBlocks, where no sound comes out for a moment
Comes and goes, often during excitement or fatigueTension, eye blinking, head movements or avoiding words

Stuttering usually begins between about 2 and 5 years old. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) notes that roughly 75 percent of children who stutter recover, while for others it continues into later childhood or adulthood.

Why It Happens

What Causes Stuttering in Children?

Stuttering is not caused by parenting, nervousness or anything you did. Research points to differences in how the brain coordinates speech, and stuttering often runs in families. Stress and excitement can make it more noticeable, but they are not the cause.

ASHA and NIDCD list factors linked to a higher chance that stuttering will continue:

  • A family member who stuttered into adulthood.
  • Stuttering that started after about age 3½.
  • Stuttering that has lasted 6 to 12 months or longer.
  • Being male (boys are more likely to keep stuttering).
  • Other speech or language concerns alongside the stuttering.

Timing

When to See a Speech-Language Pathologist

Consider an evaluation if:

  • Your child has stuttered for about 6 months or more.
  • You hear part-word repetitions, stretched sounds or blocks often.
  • You see tension, struggle, or body movements while your child talks.
  • Your child seems upset about talking, avoids words, or says "I can't say it."
  • Any of the risk factors above apply.
  • You're worried. Your concern is reason enough.

An evaluation doesn't commit you to therapy. Sometimes the best plan is to monitor with guidance and recheck in a few months.

At Home

How Parents Can Help a Child Who Stutters

  • Listen to what, not how. Keep eye contact and respond to your child's message.
  • Don't finish their words or tell them to "slow down" or "take a breath." Well meant, but it adds pressure.
  • Slow your own pace. Speaking a little more slowly and pausing before you answer models relaxed talking.
  • Make time for calm one-on-one talk each day, with fewer quick-fire questions.
  • Talk about it openly if your child brings it up. "That word got stuck. That happens. I'm listening."

Older Children

Stuttering in School-Age Children and Teens

When stuttering continues into the school years, the focus often widens beyond speech itself. ASHA notes that older children who stutter may start to avoid certain words, swap in easier ones, stay quiet in class, or skip activities they would otherwise enjoy. Teasing and worry about speaking in front of others can affect self-esteem.

Helpful steps for this age include:

  • Talking with your child's teacher about giving extra time to answer, not asking your child to "slow down," and handling teasing firmly.
  • Letting your child decide how they want to handle oral presentations or reading aloud, with options planned ahead.
  • Connecting with others who stutter. Groups such as the Stuttering Foundation offer resources for kids, teens and families, and knowing they're not alone often matters a lot to children.
  • Asking about school services. Fluency is one of the speech and language areas school teams can assess through the IEP process.

For teens, therapy often includes learning about stuttering, practicing techniques in real-life situations like ordering food or phone calls, and building confidence to speak up regardless of fluency.

Speech Therapy

How Stuttering Therapy for Kids Works

A licensed speech-language pathologist listens to your child talk in different situations, counts and describes the types of disfluency, and asks how stuttering affects your child's feelings and daily life. Therapy is then matched to your child's age and needs. ASHA describes several evidence-informed approaches an SLP may draw on:

  • Parent-led programs for preschoolers. For example, the Lidcombe Program trains parents to give gentle, positive feedback on smooth speech during short daily practice.
  • Indirect approaches that change the speaking environment, such as slower family pace and more turn-taking.
  • Fluency shaping, which teaches easier ways of starting and moving through words.
  • Stuttering modification, which helps older children stutter more easily and with less tension, and reduces fear and avoidance.

Questions

Childhood Stuttering: Frequently Asked Questions

Will my child outgrow stuttering?

Many do. NIDCD estimates about 75 percent of children recover. Risk factors like family history and stuttering lasting more than 6 to 12 months make it more likely to continue.

Did I cause my child's stuttering?

No. Stuttering is linked to how the brain coordinates speech and often runs in families. Parenting does not cause it, though a calm speaking environment can help.

What age is best to start stuttering therapy?

There's no single best age. Preschool programs can work well, and older children and teens benefit too. An evaluation helps decide whether to start now or monitor.

Should I tell my child to slow down?

It's better to slow your own speech and give your child time. Direct advice like "slow down" can make children more aware and tense.
