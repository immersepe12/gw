Step by Step

What Happens at a Speech Evaluation

Evaluations vary with your child's age and concerns, but most include these parts:

1. Talking with you

The speech-language pathologist (SLP) asks about pregnancy and birth, health, ear infections, hearing, milestones, languages spoken at home, and what you're noticing. You know your child best, so this part matters.

2. Play and observation

Especially with toddlers, much of the evaluation looks like play. The SLP watches how your child communicates: words, sounds, gestures, eye gaze, and how they take turns and share attention.

3. Standardized testing

For many children, the SLP uses tests that compare your child's skills with those of other children the same age, such as naming pictures or following directions. These tests are one piece of the picture, not the whole story.

4. Speech sounds, fluency and voice

The SLP listens to how clearly your child says sounds, whether speech flows smoothly, and how their voice sounds. They may look at how the lips and tongue move.

5. Hearing

Hearing affects speech. If your child hasn't had a recent hearing test, the SLP may suggest one through your pediatrician or an audiologist.

6. Results and next steps

You'll hear what the SLP found and what they recommend. A written report usually follows.

Who does the evaluation

At our practice, evaluations are done by licensed speech-language pathologists. In California, SLPs are licensed by the Speech-Language Pathology and Audiology and Hearing Aid Dispensers Board, which requires a graduate degree, supervised clinical experience and a national exam. If your child is also being seen by our pediatric physical or occupational therapists, or by our physician, findings can be shared within a single care plan with your permission.

Evaluating children who speak more than one language

If your family speaks Spanish, Punjabi or another language at home, tell us when you book. ASHA advises looking at a child's skills across all the languages they use, because a child may know some words in one language and other words in another. Learning two languages does not cause a speech or language disorder, and you don't need to stop using your home language.

An evaluation commonly takes about an hour, sometimes longer for older children or more complex concerns. Children don't have to "perform" perfectly. SLPs expect shyness and short attention spans and plan around them.

Before the Visit

How to Prepare Your Child and Yourself

  • Write down your concerns and examples, like words your child uses or sounds they leave out.
  • Record a short video of your child talking or playing at home. Children often talk less in a new place.
  • Bring reports from your pediatrician, hearing tests, Early Start, Regional Center or your child's school, including any IEP.
  • Bring comfort items: a favorite toy, snack and drink, glasses or hearing aids if used.
  • Keep it simple for your child. "We're going to play with someone who helps kids with talking" works well.
  • Pick a good time of day, avoiding nap time when possible.

After the Evaluation

Understanding the Results

Results usually lead to one of three paths:

  • Your child is on track. You leave with reassurance and ideas to keep language growing.
  • Watch and recheck. Your child is close to expected ranges, and the SLP suggests home strategies and another look in a few months.
  • Therapy is recommended. The SLP explains the goals, how often sessions might happen, and how you'll be involved.

The report may describe your child's skills with terms like "receptive language," "expressive language," "speech sound disorder" or "fluency." Ask the SLP to explain anything that's unclear. You can share the report with your pediatrician and school if you choose.

If your child is under 3, you can also ask California Early Start for a free evaluation through Valley Mountain Regional Center. If your child is 3 or older, you can ask your school district in writing for a free assessment. Read private vs. school speech therapy for how these routes compare.

Questions

Speech Evaluations: Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a speech evaluation take?

Often about an hour, and sometimes longer depending on your child's age and concerns. A written report usually follows.

What if my child won't talk during the evaluation?

That's common. SLPs use play, your report and home videos to understand your child. A follow-up visit can be added if needed.

Should I stay in the room?

Usually yes, especially for young children. Your SLP may ask you to join the play or simply watch.

Can I get a free speech evaluation?

Children under 3 can be evaluated for free through California Early Start, and children 3 and older through their school district.
