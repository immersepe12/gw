The Core Toolkit

Five Talking Strategies Speech Therapists Teach Parents

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) notes that children learn language through everyday back-and-forth with the people who love them. These five strategies make those moments count:

1. Self-talk

Describe what you are doing as you do it, in short, simple phrases. Making lunch: "Open bread. Spread peanut butter. Cut, cut, cut. Yum!" Your child hears words matched to actions.

2. Parallel talk

Describe what your child is doing. If they are stacking blocks: "Blue block. Up, up. Oh, it fell down!" You add words to their interest without asking them to perform.

3. Expansion

Repeat what your child says and add a little more. Child: "Dog." You: "Big dog!" Child: "Dog run." You: "The dog is running fast." Aim one step ahead of where they are.

4. Wait time

After you ask a question or start a game, pause and count slowly to five in your head. Many children need more time to respond than adults give. The silence is an invitation.

5. Follow their lead

Talk about what your child is already looking at or playing with. Children learn words best when they are interested, so the toy truck they chose beats the puzzle you picked.

Everyday Moments

Turning Daily Routines Into Practice

Routines repeat the same words every day, which is exactly how children learn them. Pick one or two and use the same short phrases each time.

  • Bath time: "Water on. Splash! Wash tummy. Wash toes. Water off. Bye-bye water." Pause before "splash" and let your child fill it in.
  • Meals and snacks: hold up two choices. "Crackers or apple?" Give a small amount so they ask for "more."
  • Getting dressed: name body parts and clothes. "Arm in. Where's your head? There it is!"
  • Car rides and walks: point out trucks, dogs and stop signs. Sing the same song and stop before the last word.
  • Bedtime: read the same favorite books. Repetition builds confidence, and familiar books make it easier for your child to join in.

Five focused minutes during a routine you already do is more realistic, and often more useful, than a long practice session.

By Age

Speech Therapy Exercises for Toddlers and Preschoolers

Every child develops at their own pace. The age ranges below follow the CDC's Learn the Signs. Act Early milestones and ASHA guidance.

12 to 18 months

Many children say a first word or two around their first birthday and use gestures like waving and pointing. Play peekaboo, name things your child points at, and copy their babbles back to them like a conversation.

18 months to 2 years

Vocabulary grows quickly, and by about 2 many children start combining two words, like "more milk." Try animal sounds, "ready, set... go!" games, and simple pretend play like feeding a doll.

2 to 3 years

Children begin using short sentences and asking simple questions. Play with toy kitchens or farm sets, talk about pictures in books, and ask open questions like "What is the bear doing?"

3 to 5 years

Kids tell short stories and use longer sentences. Retell a favorite book together, play "I spy," sort toys by color or size, and talk about what happened today in order: first, next, last.

Sound Play

Simple Games for Speech Sounds

  • Mirror faces. Sit in front of a mirror and make silly sounds together: "mmm," "buh-buh," "sss" like a snake.
  • Sound bag. Fill a bag with toys that start with the same sound, like ball, bear and boat, and take turns pulling one out.
  • Bubbles and whistles. Blowing bubbles builds lip rounding and breath control, and "pop!" is a great early word.
  • Rhyme time. Read rhyming books and leave off the rhyme: "Brown bear, brown bear, what do you..."
  • Model, do not correct. If your child says "tat" for "cat," say "Yes, a cat! A soft cat," stressing the sound gently.

Some sounds, such as r, s, l and th, often come later, sometimes not until early school years. If you are worried about clarity, read about articulation disorders.

Common Pitfalls

What to Skip

  • Quizzing. Too many "What's this? What color?" questions can feel like a test. Comment more than you ask.
  • Demanding words. Holding a snack until your child says the word often adds stress. Model the word and hand it over.
  • Background TV. The AAP recommends limiting screen time for young children and notes that live interaction matters most for language.
  • Talking for your child every time someone asks them a question. Give them a chance first.

Next Steps

When Home Practice Is Not Enough

Home activities help every child, but they do not replace an evaluation. Talk with your pediatrician and consider a speech evaluation if your child:

  • Is not babbling or using gestures like pointing by about 12 months
  • Is not combining two words by about 2 years
  • Is hard for family to understand by about 3 years
  • Loses words or skills they used to have
  • Seems frustrated often because they cannot get their message across

Free help is available. For children under 3, contact California Early Start through your regional center; in Stanislaus County that is Valley Mountain Regional Center. For children 3 and older, ask your school district for an evaluation, which can lead to an IEP. Questions

Speech Activities at Home: Frequently Asked Questions

How much time should we practice each day?

There is no magic number. Short, frequent moments woven into routines tend to work better than one long session. Try a few minutes during bath time, meals and bedtime reading. If your child is in speech therapy, ask the speech-language pathologist which goals to focus on so home practice matches therapy.

Will speaking Spanish at home confuse my child?

No. ASHA states that learning two languages does not cause speech or language problems. Children can learn two or more languages well. Speak the language you know best so your child hears rich, natural language. Mixing languages in one sentence is also a normal part of bilingual development and is nothing to worry about.

Are apps or videos good for speech?

Live conversation with a caring adult matters most. The AAP recommends very limited screen time for toddlers and co-viewing when screens are used. If you do use a show or app, watch together and talk about it. Screens should not replace face-to-face play, reading and everyday conversation with your child.

Should I correct my child when they say a word wrong?

Instead of saying "No, say it right," repeat the word back the correct way in a natural reply. If your child says "wabbit," you might say "Yes, a rabbit! The rabbit hops." This gives a clear model without making your child feel they failed, which keeps talking fun and low-pressure.

Can home activities replace speech therapy?

They help every child, but they are not a substitute for an evaluation if you are worried. A speech-language pathologist can find out what is going on and build a plan. In good therapy, parents are coached to use strategies at home, so these activities and professional support work together.
