The Basics

What Is Childhood Apraxia of Speech?

Speaking takes split-second planning. The brain has to send precise instructions to the lips, jaw and tongue, in the right order and timing, for every sound. In childhood apraxia of speech, that planning and programming step does not work smoothly. The muscles themselves are not weak; the trouble is getting the message to them in the right sequence.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) describes CAS as a neurological speech sound disorder. In most children, no clear cause is found. It sometimes occurs with genetic conditions, other neurological conditions or known brain injury, and it can run in families.

CAS is considered uncommon. ASHA notes that published prevalence estimates are limited and vary, with an often-cited estimate of about 1 to 2 children per 1,000. Many children who are late to talk do not have apraxia, which is why a careful evaluation matters.

For Parents

Apraxia Symptoms in Toddlers and Young Children

No single sign confirms CAS, and many of these can occur in other speech problems. Parents and SLPs often notice a pattern like this:

In babies and toddlers

  • Little babbling or cooing as a baby, or babble with few different sounds.
  • First words come late, and a child may say a word once and then not be able to say it again.
  • Understanding language much better than they can speak.
  • Relying heavily on pointing, gestures or grunting.

In preschool and older children

  • Inconsistent errors: the same word sounds different each time it is said.
  • Groping, visible searching movements of the lips or tongue when trying to talk.
  • Longer words and phrases fall apart more than short ones.
  • Choppy, flat or unusual rhythm and stress, so speech can sound robotic.
  • Vowel errors, which are less common in other speech disorders.
  • Easier speech in automatic phrases like "hi" or songs than when asked to say something on purpose.

Some children with CAS also have trouble with feeding, fine motor skills, reading or spelling later on. If your child is simply late to talk, our page on speech delay in toddlers covers milestone ranges.

Telling Them Apart

Apraxia vs Speech Delay, Dysarthria and Phonological Disorder

  • Speech delay: a child follows the usual pattern of speech development but more slowly. Errors are typically consistent and predictable for their age. Many late talkers catch up; children with CAS usually need direct, ongoing therapy.
  • Dysarthria: speech is affected by weakness, stiffness or poor control of the muscles themselves, often linked to a neurological condition. Speech may sound slurred, soft or breathy, and it tends to be affected consistently, not just with planning harder words.
  • Phonological disorder: a child uses predictable sound patterns, such as dropping the ends of words ("ca" for "cat") or swapping sounds ("tar" for "car"). These are rule-based errors, not movement-planning problems.
  • Articulation disorder: trouble making particular sounds correctly, like "r" or "s." See articulation therapy for more.

Children can have more than one of these at the same time, which is another reason an evaluation by a licensed speech-language pathologist is important.

Evaluation

How a Speech-Language Pathologist Diagnoses CAS

CAS is diagnosed by a speech-language pathologist (SLP), sometimes working with a neurologist or developmental pediatrician. There is no single test. The SLP looks at how your child produces sounds, syllables and words across many tasks, including repeating words several times, saying longer words, and the rhythm and stress of their speech. They also check oral motor skills and how well your child understands language.

It is often hard to make a confident diagnosis before about age 3, especially if a child says very few words. In younger children, an SLP may describe "suspected CAS" and start therapy right away while watching how speech develops. A hearing test is recommended to rule out hearing loss.

At Our Practice

How Speech Therapy for Apraxia Works

Because CAS is a movement-planning problem, ASHA notes that therapy usually focuses on practicing the movements of speech many times, with feedback. Common elements include:

  • Lots of practice. Many repetitions of carefully chosen words in each session, since frequent, intensive practice tends to help most.
  • Multisensory cues. Watching the therapist's mouth, touch cues and slowed-down speech, faded as your child improves.
  • Whole words and phrases that matter to your child, rather than isolated sounds alone.
  • Other ways to communicate, such as signs, pictures or a speech device, so your child can express themselves while speech develops. These do not stop children from learning to talk.
  • Home practice. Short daily practice with you, guided by the SLP.

Progress with CAS is often slower than with other speech problems, and every child's path is different. Questions

Childhood Apraxia of Speech: Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between apraxia and a speech delay?

A child with a speech delay follows the usual path of speech development, just more slowly, and errors tend to be consistent. In apraxia, the brain has trouble planning speech movements, so errors are often inconsistent, longer words are harder, and rhythm sounds unusual. Only a speech-language pathologist can tell them apart with confidence.

At what age can apraxia be diagnosed?

A firm diagnosis is often hard before about age 3, because a child needs enough speech for the SLP to see patterns. Younger children may be described as having suspected apraxia. That is not a reason to wait: therapy can start right away, and the SLP will confirm or revise the picture as your child's speech develops.

Will my child with apraxia learn to talk?

Many children with CAS make meaningful progress with frequent, focused therapy, but how much and how fast varies widely, and no one can promise a specific outcome. Children with other conditions alongside CAS may need more support. Your SLP will set realistic goals and update you regularly on how your child is doing.

Will sign language or a speech device stop my child from talking?

No. Research has not found that signs, picture systems or speech devices hold back speech, and they can lower frustration by giving your child a way to communicate now. ASHA supports using these tools alongside speech practice. Many children naturally use them less as their spoken words become easier to say.

How often does a child with apraxia need speech therapy?

Children with CAS generally do better with frequent sessions and lots of practice than with occasional visits. ASHA notes that individual sessions several times a week are often recommended, especially early on. Your SLP will suggest a schedule based on your child's needs, plus short practice at home on the days in between.
