The Basics

What Is Pediatric Physical Therapy?

Pediatric physical therapy focuses on how a child moves. A licensed physical therapist looks at strength, muscle tone, balance, coordination and the "big" movements doctors call gross motor skills: holding up the head, rolling, sitting, crawling, standing, walking, climbing stairs, running and jumping.

For a baby, therapy often looks like guided play on a mat, with stretches and positioning ideas for you to use at home. For an older child, it can look like obstacle courses, ball games and balance challenges. The play is purposeful: each activity is chosen to practice a skill your child is working toward.

Pediatric PT is different from occupational therapy, which focuses on fine motor skills, self-care and sensory needs, and from speech therapy, which focuses on communication. Many children benefit from more than one. Because all three are offered within one physician-led practice, your child's therapists can share a single care plan. See occupational therapy for kids and adults and pediatric speech therapy.

For Parents

Signs Your Child May Benefit From an Evaluation

Every child develops at their own pace, and milestones happen across a range of ages, not on one exact date. The CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." program lists milestones that most children (about 75%) reach by a certain age. If your child has not reached them, that is a reason to talk to your pediatrician, not a diagnosis.

Other reasons parents ask about physical therapy:

  • Your baby always turns the head to one side or has a flat spot on the head.
  • Your child walks on tiptoes most of the time after age 2 or so.
  • Your child seems much stiffer or much floppier than other children the same age.
  • Your child uses one side of the body much more than the other.
  • Your child falls far more often than peers, tires quickly or avoids climbing and running.
  • Your child has lost a movement skill they used to have. This needs a call to your pediatrician promptly.

Common Reasons

What Pediatric PT Can Help With

Motor milestone delays

Some children are slower to roll, sit, crawl or walk. Therapy builds the strength and balance behind those skills through play and gives you simple activities for home.

Torticollis and head-shape concerns

Congenital muscular torticollis means a tight neck muscle keeps a baby's head tilted or turned. Stretching, positioning and tummy-time strategies are the usual first approach, and guidelines from the American Physical Therapy Association note that starting early tends to go better.

Toe walking

Many toddlers walk on their toes for a while. When it continues, therapy can work on calf flexibility, strength and walking patterns, alongside your pediatrician's evaluation of the cause.

Balance, coordination and strength

Some school-age children struggle with running, jumping, catching or keeping up on the playground. Therapy can build those skills and your child's confidence.

Recovery after injury or surgery

Kids get sprains, fractures and sports injuries too. When a child's doctor clears them to start, therapy can help them regain motion and strength step by step.

Pediatric PT does not diagnose conditions such as cerebral palsy or autism. Diagnosis comes from your child's physician or a specialist. Therapy works on the movement skills your child needs, whatever the underlying reason.

What to Expect

What Pediatric Therapy Sessions Look Like

  • An evaluation first. The therapist asks about pregnancy, birth, health history and your concerns, then watches your child move and play, and checks strength, range of motion and balance.
  • Goals you help set. Goals are about real life, such as sitting to play with toys, walking at daycare or climbing the slide.
  • Parents in the room. You learn the activities alongside your child, because most practice happens at home between visits.
  • A short home program. A few activities that fit into diaper changes, bath time or playtime, not hours of exercises.
  • Regular progress checks, with updates shared with your pediatrician when you give permission.

How often and how long your child comes depends on their needs, and the plan changes as they progress. No one can promise a timeline, because every child develops differently. We will tell you honestly what we see and when therapy is no longer needed.

Know Your Options

Free Evaluation Routes Every Parent Should Know

You do not have to start with a private clinic. Two public programs offer free evaluations, and we think every parent should know about them.

Under age 3: California Early Start

California Early Start provides free developmental evaluations, and services for eligible infants and toddlers from birth to 36 months. In Stanislaus County, Early Start is reached through Valley Mountain Regional Center. You can contact them directly; you do not need a doctor's referral.

Age 3 and older: your school district

Under the federal Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), public school districts must find and evaluate children who may need special education, at no cost to families. You can request an evaluation in writing from your district. If your child qualifies, services are written into an Individualized Education Program (IEP).

Why some families also choose a clinic

School-based therapy focuses on what a child needs to take part in school. Some families want additional help, a medical view of their child's movement, or care while they wait for a public evaluation. Clinic therapy can work alongside Early Start or an IEP, and we are glad to coordinate with your child's other providers when you give permission.

Questions

Pediatric Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Does my child need a referral for pediatric physical therapy?

California law allows physical therapy without a referral for a limited time, but some health plans still require one. Call us and we will explain the steps for your situation. Talking with your pediatrician first is always a good idea.

At what age can a child start physical therapy?

Physical therapy can start in early infancy, for example for torticollis. There is no minimum age; what matters is whether an evaluation shows your child would benefit.

Is pediatric PT the same as occupational therapy?

No. Physical therapy focuses on large movements like walking and balance. Occupational therapy focuses on hand skills, self-care and sensory needs. Some children benefit from both.

Is there free physical therapy for babies in California?

California Early Start offers free evaluations, and services for eligible children from birth to 36 months, through regional centers. In Stanislaus County that is Valley Mountain Regional Center.

How long will my child need therapy?

It depends on your child's needs and progress, so no one can promise a timeline. The therapist reviews progress regularly and adjusts or ends the plan when goals are met.
