The Basics

What a Venous Leg Ulcer Is

Your leg veins have small one-way valves that help push blood back up to the heart. When those valves are weak or damaged, blood pools in the lower legs. This is called chronic venous insufficiency. The extra pressure causes swelling, and over time the skin around the ankle becomes thin, discolored and fragile. A small bump or scratch can then turn into an open sore that is slow to heal. That sore is a venous leg ulcer, sometimes called a venous stasis ulcer.

Venous ulcers are the most common type of leg ulcer. They usually form on the inner side of the leg just above the ankle. They tend to be shallow, with uneven edges, and often weep fluid.

How they differ from other leg wounds

Not every leg sore is venous. Ulcers caused by poor artery blood flow (arterial ulcers) often sit on the toes or outer ankle, look "punched out," and hurt more when the legs are raised. Diabetic foot ulcers usually form on pressure points of the foot. The treatment for each is different, and compression can be harmful when artery blood flow is poor. That is why a proper assessment comes first.

Know the Signs

Signs and Risk Factors

Signs of venous disease and ulcers

  • Swollen ankles or lower legs, often worse at the end of the day
  • Aching, heavy or tired legs
  • Brown or reddish skin discoloration near the ankle
  • Dry, itchy, scaly skin (venous eczema), or hardened skin
  • Varicose veins
  • An open sore that weeps fluid and doesn't heal

Who is more likely to get them

  • People with varicose veins or a past blood clot in the leg (deep vein thrombosis)
  • Older adults
  • People with obesity
  • People who stand or sit for long periods at work
  • People with a past leg injury or surgery, or several pregnancies
  • People with limited ankle movement, which weakens the calf "pump"

Treatment

How Venous Leg Ulcers Are Treated

1. Checking blood flow first

Before compression, a clinician checks the pulses in your feet and often compares blood pressure at the ankle and arm (the ankle-brachial index). This makes sure artery flow is good enough for compression to be safe. Some people also need an ultrasound of the leg veins and referral to a vein specialist.

2. Compression therapy

Compression is the main treatment for venous ulcers. Wraps, multi-layer bandages or compression stockings squeeze the leg to reduce swelling and help blood move upward. Compression only works if it is worn as directed, so we help find an option you can put on and tolerate.

3. Wound care

The ulcer is cleaned and dressed with materials chosen to manage drainage and protect the surrounding skin. Dead tissue may be gently removed. Signs of infection are treated, and the wound is measured at each visit to track progress.

4. Elevation and movement

Raising your legs above heart level several times a day helps swelling. Walking and simple ankle exercises strengthen the calf muscle pump. Physical therapy can help if walking is hard.

Living with compression

Many people find compression hard at first. Stockings can be tight to put on, warm in a Central Valley summer, and uncomfortable over a sore leg. Tell us if that is happening rather than quietly stopping. Options include different compression levels, wraps with adjustable straps, and devices that help you pull stockings on. Putting them on first thing in the morning, before swelling builds, is usually easiest. If you have trouble bending or reaching your feet, a family member or a physical or occupational therapist can help you find a routine that works.

Pain and itching

Venous ulcers can ache, and the skin around them often itches. Elevation and compression usually help both over time. Avoid scratching, which can open new sores. Your clinician can suggest skin care for the surrounding area and talk with you about safe pain relief.

5. Treating the cause

Some people benefit from procedures on the faulty veins, done by vein specialists. We refer when that may help.

Hyperbaric Oxygen

Is Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Used for Venous Ulcers?

No. Venous leg ulcers are not an HBOT indication. They are not on the Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society's list of accepted indications, and Medicare's national coverage policy does not include them. The core problem is pressure and swelling in the veins, which compression addresses directly.

Although our practice has hyperbaric chambers, we will not recommend HBOT for a venous ulcer. If a leg wound turns out to have a different cause, such as a diabetic foot ulcer or radiation injury, your physician will explain whether HBOT is appropriate. See which wounds HBOT is used for.

After Healing

Keeping Venous Ulcers From Coming Back

Venous ulcers often come back, because the vein problem underneath is still there. These habits lower the chance:

  • Keep wearing compression stockings as prescribed, often for life
  • Moisturize the skin on your lower legs daily
  • Walk regularly and avoid standing or sitting still for long stretches
  • Elevate your legs when resting
  • Protect your legs from bumps and scratches
  • Check your legs often and call early if the skin breaks down

Questions

Venous Leg Ulcers: Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best treatment for a venous leg ulcer?

Compression, combined with good wound care, leg elevation and walking, is the foundation of treatment once blood flow has been checked. A clinician checks the pulses in your feet and often compares blood pressure at the ankle and arm first. This makes sure artery flow is good enough for compression to be safe.

How long does a venous ulcer take to heal?

Many take weeks to months. Size, how long the ulcer has been open, and how consistently compression is worn all affect healing. Keep up with compression, leg elevation and walking between visits. Get same-day care if you see spreading redness, warmth, red streaks, fever, a foul smell or black tissue.

Can hyperbaric oxygen help a venous ulcer?

Venous ulcers are not an HBOT indication, and we do not offer HBOT for them. Compression treats the underlying problem. Venous ulcers come from leg veins that don't move blood back to the heart well. Good wound care, leg elevation and staying active support that treatment.

Should I keep my leg up or walk?

Both. Walking works the calf pump, and raising your legs when resting reduces swelling. Avoid standing still for long periods. Limited ankle movement weakens the calf pump, so staying active matters. Standing or sitting for long periods at work is also a risk factor for these ulcers.

Are venous leg ulcers serious?

They can be. A venous leg ulcer is rarely an emergency, but it is often slow to heal and tends to come back without ongoing care such as compression. Left untreated, it can become infected. See a clinician for any leg wound that has not started healing within about two weeks, and get same-day care for spreading redness, fever, foul odor or black tissue.
