In Brief

The Short Answer

For the conditions HBOT is commonly used for in outpatient centers, a typical course is 20 to 40 sessions, scheduled once a day, five days a week. That works out to roughly four to eight weeks. Emergencies are different: they are treated in hospitals, and the number of sessions is decided there. Your physician sets your number, and reviews it as you go.

Why HBOT is a course, not a single dose

A single session raises the oxygen in your blood and tissues for a few hours. That alone does not rebuild damaged tissue. The changes HBOT is used to support in chronic conditions, such as the growth of new small blood vessels in tissue with poor circulation, build up gradually over weeks of repeated sessions. That is why consistency across the whole course matters more than any single visit, and why no one should expect a chronic wound or radiation injury to look different after a handful of sessions.

Frequency

How Often: Daily, Five Days a Week

Yes, you can do hyperbaric oxygen therapy every day, and for planned courses that is the norm: one session a day, five days a week, with weekends off. There are good reasons for that rhythm.

  • The effect builds with repetition. Regular sessions keep the process moving; long gaps slow it.
  • Your body needs recovery time. Oxygen under pressure is dosed carefully, and a daily schedule keeps total exposure within safe limits.
  • It is realistic. A weekday routine fits around work and family, and gives weekends to rest.

Each Visit

How Long Each Session Takes

A typical session runs about 90 to 120 minutes, including the time spent gradually pressurizing and depressurizing the chamber. For wound and radiation indications, published protocols commonly use pressures of 2.0 to 2.5 times normal atmospheric pressure, and 2.4 times normal pressure for 90 minutes is a classic protocol. At Generations Wellness, you breathe medical-grade oxygen in a hard-shell monoplace chamber at the pressure your physician prescribes.

Progress

When Will You Know If It's Helping?

It depends on the condition, and no outcome is promised. What you can expect is a structured way of checking.

  • Diabetic foot ulcers and chronic wounds. Progress is judged by measurable change in the wound over weeks, which is why Medicare builds in a formal review every 30 days. In the Löndahl 2010 trial, the difference in complete healing was measured at one year, not during the course itself.
  • Radiation injury. Changes in symptoms such as bleeding are typically assessed over the course and afterward. The RICH-ART trial measured urinary symptom scores after treatment.
  • Sudden hearing loss. Hearing is checked with hearing tests during and after treatment, and starting early matters.
  • Grafts and flaps. Surgeons watch the tissue closely, often over a much shorter time frame.

Just as important: re-evaluation is how a course that is not working gets stopped. Your physician reviews your progress and will tell you honestly if continuing does not make sense.

Real Life

Missed Sessions and Breaks

An occasional missed day is normal. What matters is the pattern over the whole course. A few things to know:

  • Illness pauses treatment. A cold, congestion or fever can make pressure changes unsafe for your ears and sinuses, so a session may be postponed.
  • Call as early as you can. That lets the schedule be adjusted.
  • Longer gaps get a physician review. Your physician decides whether sessions should be added or the plan changed.

Coverage Rules

Insurance Rules That Shape the Count

At facilities that bill insurance, coverage rules can influence how a course is structured. It helps to understand them even if you are paying yourself.

  • Medicare covers specific indications only. National Coverage Determination 20.29 lists 15 covered conditions.
  • Diabetic foot ulcers have strict criteria. The wound must be Wagner grade 3 or higher, and must have shown no measurable signs of healing after at least 30 days of standard wound care.
  • Progress is checked every 30 days. Coverage ends if there is no measurable healing in any 30-day period.
  • Private plans often require prior authorization, including many Medicare Advantage plans.

Budgeting

Doing the Math on Cost

Because HBOT is a course, the total is the number to plan around: sessions multiplied by the price per session. At the national range of $150 to $450 per session, a 20-session course comes to roughly $3,000 to $9,000 and a 40-session course to roughly $6,000 to $18,000, depending on the setting. Those are ranges, not quotes.

Questions

HBOT Sessions: Frequently Asked Questions

How many HBOT sessions will I need?

Most outpatient courses are 20 to 40 daily sessions. Your physician sets the number for your condition and reviews progress along the way.

Can you do hyperbaric oxygen therapy every day?

Yes. One session a day, five days a week, is the typical schedule for a planned course.

How long is one session?

About 90 to 120 minutes, including gradual pressurization and depressurization. Your physician sets the exact pressure and duration.

How many sessions for a diabetic foot ulcer?

It varies; a well-known double-blind trial used 40 sessions. Medicare coverage requires a Wagner grade 3 or higher ulcer, at least 30 days of failed standard care, and re-evaluation every 30 days.

How soon do you see results?

It depends on the condition. Chronic wounds are judged over weeks, often at 30-day reviews, and no specific outcome can be promised.

What if I miss a session?

An occasional missed day is fine. Call us as early as you can so your schedule can be adjusted, and your physician will review longer gaps.
