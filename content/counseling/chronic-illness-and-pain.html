Mind and Body

Why Counseling Has a Place in Pain and Illness Care

Pain is real, and so is the toll it takes. Living for months or years with back pain, arthritis, neuropathy, diabetes, a slow-healing wound or another long-term condition affects sleep, mood, work, money and relationships. Low mood and worry, in turn, can make pain feel stronger and make it harder to stay active. Many people get caught in that loop.

Counseling works on the parts of the loop you can influence: how you pace activity, how you sleep, how you respond to flare-ups, and how you handle the frustration, fear and grief that often come with illness. It is used alongside your medical care, never instead of it.

The CDC's 2022 clinical practice guideline for prescribing opioids for pain notes that non-drug treatments, including cognitive behavioral therapy, are preferred options for many types of chronic pain, often combined with exercise and other care.

Approaches

CBT for Chronic Pain and Other Approaches

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for chronic pain

CBT for pain teaches practical skills: pacing activity to avoid boom-and-bust cycles, relaxation and breathing to ease muscle tension, problem-solving, improving sleep, and working with thoughts such as "this will never get better" that add to distress. A 2020 Cochrane review of psychological therapies for chronic pain (not including headache) found CBT had small benefits for pain, disability and distress compared with usual care. The benefits were modest and varied between people, so it is best seen as one helpful part of a broader plan.

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)

ACT helps you stop putting life on hold until pain goes away. You clarify what matters to you and take steps toward it, while learning to make room for discomfort.

Adjusting to a diagnosis

A new or serious diagnosis can bring shock, fear and grief for the life you expected. Counseling offers a place to process that, make decisions about treatment, and talk about changes in roles at home or work. See also grief and life transitions counseling.

Talking with family and your care team

Chronic conditions change roles at home and can strain relationships. Counseling can help you explain your limits to family, ask for help, and prepare for medical appointments so you can say what matters most to you in the time you have with your doctors.

Sleep, stress and mood

Poor sleep, depression and anxiety are common with chronic illness and can each make the others worse. Your counselor can work on all three. Read more about counseling for depression and counseling for anxiety.

Who It May Help

Conditions People Often Bring to Counseling

  • Long-lasting back, neck or joint pain, and arthritis
  • Nerve pain and neuropathy
  • Fibromyalgia and headaches
  • Diabetes, heart disease and other conditions that need daily self-care
  • Slow-healing wounds and the long weeks of care they require
  • Recovery after surgery, injury or a hospital stay
  • Caring for a family member with a serious illness

Counseling does not treat the disease itself, and results differ from person to person. The aim is to help you cope better and live more fully while you and your doctors manage the condition.

Questions

Counseling for Pain and Illness: Frequently Asked Questions

Does being referred to counseling mean my pain is not real?

No. Pain is real. Counseling helps with the stress, sleep, mood and daily-life effects that come with it, alongside your medical care.

Does CBT work for chronic pain?

Research shows small, real benefits on average for pain, disability and distress. It works best as part of a broader plan that may include physical therapy and medical care.

Can I see a counselor if I am already seeing a pain doctor elsewhere?

Yes. With your permission, we can coordinate with your other doctors.

Can I do sessions from home?

Yes, by secure telehealth, as long as you are in California during the session.

How can counseling help with chronic pain?

Counseling does not replace medical care for pain, but it can help you cope with how pain affects your life. A licensed counselor may use skills from approaches like cognitive behavioral therapy and acceptance-based therapy, such as pacing activity, managing stress and sleep, and handling hard thoughts. Many people find this useful alongside physical therapy and medical treatment, though results vary from person to person.
