Understanding Grief

What Grief Can Look Like

Grief is the natural response to loss. It is not a problem to be fixed, and there is no right way or set timeline to do it. MedlinePlus describes grief as a reaction that can include shock, numbness, sadness, anger, guilt, and relief, sometimes all in the same day.

Grief also shows up in the body and in daily life:

  • Trouble sleeping, or sleeping much more than usual
  • Changes in appetite and low energy
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
  • Waves of intense emotion triggered by anniversaries, songs or places
  • Pulling away from people, or not wanting to be alone

The older idea of fixed "stages of grief" is now seen as a loose description rather than a set path. Many people move back and forth between missing the person and getting on with daily life, and over time the balance usually shifts.

When to Reach Out

When Grief Counseling Can Help

You do not need to be "stuck" to benefit from talking with a counselor. People often reach out when:

  • They do not have someone they can talk to openly, or feel they have to stay strong for others
  • The loss was sudden, traumatic, or complicated by conflict or guilt
  • Months have passed and the pain is not easing, or daily life is still hard to manage
  • Grief has turned into deep hopelessness, or they are using alcohol or other substances to cope
  • They are caring for others who are grieving, such as children or an aging parent

For a small number of people, intense longing and preoccupation with the person who died continue for a year or more and make daily life very hard. Clinicians call this prolonged grief disorder. If that sounds familiar, an assessment with a licensed clinician is a good next step.

If grief brings thoughts of wanting to die or join the person you lost, please call or text 988 now.

Life Changes

Counseling for Big Life Transitions

Loss is not only about death. Many changes bring grief for the life you had or expected. Counseling can help with:

  • Divorce, separation or the end of a long relationship
  • Retirement, a job loss or a career change
  • Becoming a caregiver for a parent or partner
  • Children leaving home, or a move away from family and community
  • A new diagnosis, surgery, or a change in what your body can do
  • Pregnancy loss or infertility

Transitions often bring a mix of feelings that do not fit neatly together: relief and sadness, excitement and fear. Counseling gives you room to hold both, sort out what you want next, and plan practical steps without feeling rushed by other people's expectations.

Even welcome changes, like a new baby or a move to a new city, can bring stress and mixed feelings. Talking it through helps many people make sense of the change and find their footing.

In Session

What Grief Counseling Involves

Grief counseling is not about "moving on" or forgetting. It is a place to tell your story, feel what you feel without judgment, and find ways to carry the loss alongside the rest of your life. Depending on what you need, your counselor may help you:

  • Talk about the person or the life you lost, and what they meant to you
  • Work through guilt, anger or unfinished business
  • Manage sleep, routines and hard days such as holidays and anniversaries
  • Reconnect with people and activities at a pace that feels right
  • Make practical decisions without rushing

Some people need a few sessions; others meet for longer. Your counselor reviews progress with you, and results differ from person to person. Free community support, such as bereavement groups offered through local hospices and faith communities, can also be a good companion to counseling.

Questions

Grief Counseling: Frequently Asked Questions

How long does grief last?

There is no set timeline. For most people the pain becomes less constant over months, though waves can return around anniversaries for years.

Is it too soon, or too late, to start grief counseling?

Neither. Some people reach out within weeks, others years later. The right time is when you feel you would like support.

What is the difference between grief and depression?

Grief tends to come in waves tied to the loss, with moments of normal feeling in between. Depression is a more constant low mood and loss of interest. They can overlap, and a clinician can help sort it out.

Can I get counseling for a life change that is not a death?

Yes. Divorce, retirement, caregiving, a move or a new diagnosis are common reasons adults start counseling.
