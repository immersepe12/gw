Understanding Anxiety

Everyday Worry vs. an Anxiety Disorder

Everyone feels anxious sometimes. Nerves before a job interview or worry about a sick parent are normal reactions that usually pass. The National Institute of Mental Health explains that for people with an anxiety disorder, the anxiety does not go away and can get worse over time, to the point that it gets in the way of work, school, relationships and daily life.

Anxiety disorders are among the most common mental health conditions. They include:

  • Generalized anxiety disorder: persistent, hard-to-control worry about many everyday things, most days, for months.
  • Panic disorder: repeated, unexpected panic attacks and fear of having another one.
  • Social anxiety disorder: intense fear of being judged or embarrassed in social or performance situations.
  • Specific phobias: strong fear of a particular thing or situation, such as driving, needles or heights.

Only a licensed clinician can make a diagnosis after an assessment. You do not need a diagnosis to start counseling, and you do not need to know which label fits.

Common Signs

Signs It May Be Time to Talk to Someone

Anxiety shows up in the mind and the body. Common signs include:

  • Feeling restless, on edge or keyed up much of the time
  • Worry that is hard to stop, even when you know it is out of proportion
  • Trouble sleeping, or waking with your mind racing
  • Muscle tension, headaches, stomach upset or a pounding heart
  • Trouble concentrating, or your mind going blank
  • Avoiding places, people or tasks because they make you anxious
  • Irritability and feeling worn out

A good rule of thumb: if anxiety has lasted for weeks, is getting in the way of things you need or want to do, or you are avoiding more and more, it is worth talking to a professional. Physical symptoms such as chest pain or shortness of breath should be checked by a doctor first, because they can have medical causes too.

Panic Attacks

What a Panic Attack Feels Like

A panic attack is a sudden wave of intense fear that peaks within minutes. People describe a racing or pounding heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, chest tightness, dizziness, and a sense of doom or losing control. Many people go to an emergency room during their first attack because it feels like a heart problem.

Panic attacks are frightening but treatable. Counseling helps you understand what happens in your body during an attack, ride out the sensations instead of fighting them, and gradually stop avoiding the places where attacks have happened.

If you have chest pain, trouble breathing or fainting and you are not sure what is causing it, call 911. It is always right to rule out a medical emergency.

Approaches

How Counseling for Anxiety Works

The NIMH notes that psychotherapy is a common, well-studied treatment for anxiety disorders, and that therapy should be tailored to the person's specific anxieties and needs. Your counselor chooses approaches that fit you. Common ones include:

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

CBT is one of the most studied approaches for anxiety. You learn to notice anxious thoughts, test how accurate they are, and replace them with more balanced ones. You also practice new behaviors, such as calming the body with slow breathing or facing situations you have been avoiding.

Exposure, step by step

For panic and phobias, counselors often use gradual exposure: approaching feared situations in small, planned steps, at a pace you agree to. Over time, the fear tends to shrink as your brain learns the situation is safer than it felt.

Acceptance-based approaches

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) and mindfulness skills help you make room for uncomfortable feelings instead of struggling with them, so you can focus on what matters to you.

Practical skills for daily life

Sessions often include sleep habits, managing caffeine, problem-solving, and planning for stressful events. Many clients practice short exercises between sessions.

How long counseling takes varies. Some people feel more in control after a handful of sessions; others work longer on deeper patterns. Your counselor will review progress with you along the way, and you can adjust the plan at any time. Results differ from person to person.

Counseling and medication

Some people use counseling alone; others combine it with medicine. Counselors do not prescribe. If you want to talk about medication, speak with a physician, such as your primary care doctor.

Questions

Anxiety Counseling: Frequently Asked Questions

Can therapy help anxiety without medication?

For many people, yes. Psychotherapy such as CBT is a well-studied treatment for anxiety disorders. Some people do best with therapy and medicine together, which is a decision to make with a physician. Counseling can help you understand what happens in your body and gradually stop avoiding the things that make you anxious.

How many sessions will I need?

It varies. Your counselor will discuss a plan at your first session and review progress with you regularly. Progress looks different for each person, and you can share questions or concerns about your plan at any visit. Together you and your counselor set goals that matter to you.

Can I have anxiety counseling by telehealth?

Yes, by secure video, as long as you are in California during the session. Some people with social anxiety find it an easier way to start. Video sessions use the same approach as in-person counseling. Your counselor can explain how a video visit works before your first session.

What should I do during a panic attack?

Slow your breathing and remind yourself the wave will pass, usually within minutes. If you have chest pain or trouble breathing and are not sure why, call 911. Panic attacks are frightening but treatable. Counseling can teach you to ride out the sensations instead of fighting them.

Do I need a referral?

No. Call us directly to start with a short screening call. You do not need a diagnosis to start counseling, and you do not need to know which label fits. On that call, we can explain how sessions work and help you plan a first appointment.
