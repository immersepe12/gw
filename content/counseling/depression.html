Understanding Depression

Sadness vs. Depression

Sadness is a normal response to loss, disappointment and hard times, and it usually eases as life moves on. Depression is different. The National Institute of Mental Health describes depression as a common but serious mood disorder that causes symptoms affecting how you feel, think and handle daily activities such as sleeping, eating and working. For a diagnosis, symptoms are typically present most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks.

Depression is not a character flaw or a lack of willpower. It can follow a stressful event, run in families, come with a medical illness or chronic pain, or appear with no clear reason. It is also treatable, and many people improve with care.

Common Signs

Signs of Depression to Watch For

  • Persistent sad, anxious or "empty" mood
  • Losing interest or pleasure in hobbies and activities
  • Feeling hopeless, guilty, worthless or helpless
  • Low energy, fatigue, or feeling slowed down
  • Sleeping too little or too much
  • Changes in appetite or weight
  • Trouble concentrating, remembering or making decisions
  • Aches, pains or digestive problems without a clear physical cause
  • Irritability, or pulling away from people
  • Thoughts of death or suicide

Not everyone has every symptom. If several have lasted two weeks or more, talk to a doctor or counselor. A physician visit is also a good idea because some medical conditions, such as thyroid problems, and some medicines can cause similar symptoms.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 now. You can also call 911. You do not have to wait for an appointment.

New Parents

Depression During and After Pregnancy

Many new mothers have the "baby blues" for a week or two after birth: tearfulness, mood swings and worry that fade on their own. Perinatal depression is more intense and lasts longer. The NIMH explains that it can start during pregnancy or after delivery and may make it hard to care for yourself or your baby. Signs include deep sadness, anxiety, feeling disconnected from the baby, and thoughts of harming yourself or the baby.

Perinatal depression is a medical condition, not a sign of being a bad parent. Talk with your obstetrician, midwife or primary care doctor about how you feel. Counseling can be part of the plan, often alongside your medical care. The National Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262), offers free, confidential support by call or text in English and Spanish, 24 hours a day.

Approaches

How Counseling for Depression Works

The NIMH lists psychotherapy, medication, or both as common treatments for depression. Your counselor chooses approaches that fit you. These include:

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

CBT helps you spot thought patterns that feed low mood, such as "nothing will ever change," and test them against the evidence. It is one of the most studied talk therapies for depression.

Behavioral activation

Depression often shrinks life down: fewer activities, less contact, less pleasure, which deepens the low mood. Behavioral activation rebuilds routines one small step at a time, scheduling activities that bring a sense of enjoyment or accomplishment.

Interpersonal therapy

This approach focuses on relationships and life events, such as conflict, loss or a change in role, and how they connect with mood.

Tracking progress

Your counselor may ask you to complete a short mood questionnaire, such as the PHQ-9, from time to time, so you can both see how things are changing. Progress is rarely a straight line, and plans change as you go. Results differ from person to person.

Small steps between sessions

Much of the change happens between appointments. Your counselor may suggest a short walk, a phone call to a friend, a regular bedtime, or writing down one thought to look at next time. These steps are agreed together and sized to your energy, so they feel doable even on hard days. If a step does not work, that is useful information, not failure.

Counseling and medication

Counselors do not prescribe. For some people, medicine prescribed by a physician is part of the plan, especially for more severe depression. If you are curious about medication, talk with a physician, such as your primary care doctor. With your permission, your counselor and physician can share a short summary so care fits together.

Questions

Depression Counseling: Frequently Asked Questions

Is counseling effective for depression?

Psychotherapy is one of the main, well-studied treatments for depression, used alone or with medication. How much it helps differs from person to person.

How do I know if it is depression or just a rough patch?

If low mood or loss of interest lasts most days for two weeks or more and affects daily life, talk to a doctor or counselor. Only an assessment can tell for sure.

Can you help with postpartum depression?

Adult clients after pregnancy are welcome to call. We recommend involving your obstetrician or primary care doctor too, and 1-833-TLC-MAMA offers support any time.

Do your counselors prescribe antidepressants?

No. Counseling here is talk therapy. Medication questions should go to a physician.

Can I do sessions by video?

Yes, through a secure telehealth platform, as long as you are in California during the session.
