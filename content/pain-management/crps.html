The Basics

What Is Complex Regional Pain Syndrome?

According to the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS), CRPS is a form of chronic pain that usually affects one limb and typically develops after an injury, such as a fracture, sprain, surgery or a period of immobilization in a cast. The pain is out of proportion to the original injury and lasts beyond the normal healing time.

Researchers think CRPS involves changes in the nerves, the immune response and the way the brain and spinal cord process signals from the limb. It is not "in your head," and it is not your fault. There are two types: CRPS-I, without a confirmed nerve injury, which is the most common, and CRPS-II, after a known nerve injury.

CRPS can happen at any age but is most common in adults around their 40s, and more often in women. Many people, especially when it is caught early, see their symptoms ease over months. For others it lasts longer, and outcomes vary.

Signs to Know

CRPS Symptoms After an Injury

Symptoms can start days to weeks after an injury and may change over time. Common ones include:

  • Burning, throbbing or stabbing pain that keeps going after the injury should have healed
  • Extreme sensitivity to touch, so even clothing, bedsheets or a breeze hurts
  • Swelling of the hand, foot or limb
  • Skin that changes color (red, purple, blotchy or pale) or temperature compared with the other side
  • Changes in sweating, hair or nail growth
  • Stiff joints, weakness, tremor or trouble moving the limb
  • A feeling that the limb does not belong to you or is hard to picture

If pain after a fracture, sprain or surgery seems to be getting worse instead of better, especially with these changes, tell your doctor soon. Get urgent care for a cold, pale or blue limb, sudden severe swelling, or signs of infection like fever and spreading redness, since these can have other serious causes.

Diagnosis

How CRPS Is Diagnosed: The Budapest Criteria

No single blood test or scan confirms CRPS. Doctors use a clinical checklist called the Budapest criteria. In plain words, a diagnosis means:

  • You have ongoing pain that is out of proportion to the original injury.
  • You report symptoms in at least three of four groups: sensation (extra sensitivity), blood flow and skin color or temperature, swelling and sweating, and movement or skin, hair and nail changes.
  • The doctor sees signs in at least two of those groups during the exam.
  • No other condition better explains the symptoms.

Tests such as X-rays, bone scans, nerve studies or blood work may be used to rule out other problems like infection, a blood clot, nerve compression or arthritis. Because other causes need to be excluded, a careful physician evaluation is an important first step.

Treatment

Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Treatment Options

There is no single fix for CRPS, and no treatment works for everyone. Care usually combines several approaches, and starting early tends to help.

Graded movement with PT and OT

Gently using the limb again is central to care. Physical and occupational therapists help you slowly rebuild movement, strength and everyday use without big flare-ups. Common approaches include:

  • Graded motor imagery. A step-by-step program that starts with recognizing left and right limbs in pictures, moves to imagining movement, then to mirror work.
  • Mirror therapy. Watching the reflection of your unaffected limb move, which may help retrain how the brain processes the painful side.
  • Desensitization. Gradually exposing the skin to different textures and touch.
  • Gentle range-of-motion, strengthening and swelling management, plus help with daily tasks such as dressing and writing. Our hand therapy page explains OT for the hand and wrist.

Medicines discussed by a physician

A physician may discuss options such as certain nerve-pain medicines, anti-inflammatory medicines, short courses of other drugs, or bone-related medicines that have been studied in CRPS. Evidence is mixed, so choices are made with you, based on your symptoms, health and other medicines. Opioids are not a recommended long-term treatment. Some nerve-pain medicines are also used for neuropathy.

Counseling support

Living with constant pain is exhausting and can bring worry, low mood and poor sleep, which in turn can make pain harder to manage. Talk therapy can teach pacing, coping and relaxation skills. See our page on counseling for chronic illness and pain.

Referral to pain specialists

When symptoms don't improve, a pain specialist may offer procedures such as sympathetic nerve blocks or, in selected cases, spinal cord stimulation. We do not perform these procedures; our physician can refer you and coordinate care.

Questions

CRPS: Frequently Asked Questions

What usually triggers CRPS?

Most cases follow an injury to an arm or leg, such as a fracture, sprain or crush injury, or surgery. A period in a cast or splint is a known risk. Less often it follows a stroke, heart attack or an injury so minor it went unnoticed. Experts don't fully understand why some people develop CRPS and others don't.

Does CRPS ever go away?

For many people, especially when treatment starts early, symptoms ease over months, and some recover fully. For others, CRPS lasts longer and needs ongoing management. Outcomes are hard to predict, and no one can promise a timeline. Keeping the limb moving gently and working with a care team gives you the best chance of improvement.

Should I rest a limb with CRPS?

Long rest usually makes CRPS worse, because the limb gets stiffer and more sensitive. Gentle, graded use is a key part of treatment. That doesn't mean pushing through severe pain. A physical or occupational therapist helps you find a starting point you can manage and build up slowly, with planned rest breaks.

What is mirror therapy for CRPS?

You place a mirror so that you see the reflection of your healthy limb where the painful one would be, then move the healthy side. Your brain sees what looks like the painful limb moving comfortably. Mirror therapy is often part of graded motor imagery, and it is done with guidance from a therapist.

Can CRPS spread to other parts of the body?

In some people, symptoms spread to a larger area of the same limb, and occasionally to another limb. This is not the usual course, though. Tell your physician about any new pain, swelling or color changes in another area, so your evaluation and treatment plan can be updated.
