The Basics

What Is Peripheral Neuropathy?

Peripheral nerves carry signals between your brain and spinal cord and the rest of your body. When they are damaged, signals can be lost, slowed or misfire. The most common pattern starts in the toes and feet and slowly moves upward, sometimes later affecting the hands. This is often called a "stocking and glove" pattern.

Common symptoms

  • Burning, stabbing or electric-shock pain, often worse at night
  • Tingling or "pins and needles"
  • Numbness, or feeling like you are wearing socks when you aren't
  • Pain from light touch, such as bedsheets on your feet
  • Poor balance or unsteadiness, especially in the dark
  • Weakness in the feet or hands

Some people mainly have pain. Others mainly have numbness, which can be risky because injuries to the feet may go unnoticed.

Why It Happens

Causes of Neuropathy and Diabetic Nerve Pain

Diabetes is the most common cause of peripheral neuropathy in the United States. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases reports that about one-third to one-half of people with diabetes have some form of nerve damage. Over time, high blood sugar harms nerves and the small blood vessels that feed them. Diabetes is common across the Central Valley, so diabetic nerve pain is something we take seriously.

Other causes include:

  • Low vitamin B12, which can be linked to long-term use of metformin or acid-reducing medicines
  • Heavy alcohol use
  • Low thyroid function or kidney disease
  • Some chemotherapy drugs and other medicines
  • Autoimmune conditions and certain infections
  • Inherited nerve conditions

For some people, no cause is found even after testing. This is called idiopathic neuropathy, and symptoms can still be managed. Finding a treatable cause early is one of the main reasons to have numbness or tingling checked rather than waiting it out, especially if symptoms are new, spreading or affecting your balance.

Testing

How Neuropathy Is Diagnosed

A physician starts with your history, medicines and an exam of your feet and hands: sensation to touch, vibration and temperature, reflexes and strength. Blood tests often check blood sugar and A1C, vitamin B12, thyroid and kidney function. Depending on the findings, nerve conduction studies or a referral to a neurologist may be needed, especially if symptoms came on quickly, affect one side only or include significant weakness.

Seek urgent care for weakness that spreads over hours to days, trouble walking or breathing, or sudden numbness on one side of the body. Call 911 for sudden one-sided weakness or numbness, which can be a sign of stroke.

Treatment

Neuropathy Treatment Options

Manage the cause

For diabetic neuropathy, keeping blood sugar in a healthy range is the most important long-term step to slow nerve damage. Correcting a low B12 level, adjusting a medicine that may be harming nerves, or cutting back on alcohol can also help when those are the cause. Nerve damage that has already occurred may not fully recover, but slowing progression matters.

Non-opioid medicines for nerve pain

The American Diabetes Association and the American Academy of Neurology list several medicine groups as options for painful diabetic neuropathy:

  • Gabapentinoids such as pregabalin or gabapentin
  • SNRIs such as duloxetine
  • Tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline, at low doses
  • Topical options such as capsaicin or lidocaine for some people

These medicines aim to reduce pain, not restore nerves. They can cause dizziness, drowsiness or other side effects, and some are not a good fit for older adults or people with certain heart conditions. Opioids are generally not recommended for neuropathic pain. A physician reviews which option, if any, fits you. Read more about non-opioid pain management.

Physical therapy and balance training

Numb feet make falls more likely. Licensed physical therapists can work on strength, balance and safe walking. See balance and fall prevention.

Coping with ongoing nerve pain

Nerve pain that disturbs sleep can wear anyone down. Counseling can help with sleep, stress and pacing. See counseling for chronic illness and pain.

Procedures and devices

For severe neuropathic pain that does not respond to other care, specialists may discuss other options. We do not offer procedures or implanted devices. If one may help, we refer you to a specialist.

Prevention

Protecting Your Feet With Neuropathy

  • Check your feet every day for cuts, blisters, redness or swelling. Use a mirror or ask for help.
  • Never walk barefoot, even at home, and check shoes for stones before putting them on.
  • Test bath water with your elbow; numb feet can't sense burns.
  • Wear well-fitting shoes and clean, dry socks.
  • Have your feet examined at least once a year, or more often if you have numbness.

A sore on the foot that is not healing, or one with spreading redness, warmth, drainage, odor or fever, needs same-day medical care. Learn about diabetic foot ulcers and our wound care services.

Clear Answers

Is Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Used for Neuropathy?

No. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) is not an FDA-cleared indication for peripheral neuropathy or diabetic nerve pain, and we do not offer HBOT to treat neuropathy. Our practice provides hyperbaric oxygen therapy only for FDA-cleared indications. One of those is certain diabetic foot ulcers that have not healed with standard wound care. That is a wound treatment, not a nerve treatment. See the conditions HBOT is cleared for.

Questions

Neuropathy: Frequently Asked Questions

What kind of doctor treats peripheral neuropathy?

Primary care and internal medicine physicians manage most neuropathy, especially diabetic neuropathy. Neurologists help when the cause is unclear, symptoms progress quickly, or specialized testing is needed.

Does neuropathy ever go away?

Sometimes symptoms improve when a cause such as low B12 is corrected. Nerve damage from long-standing diabetes often does not fully recover, but managing blood sugar can slow it and medicines can ease pain.

What helps diabetic nerve pain at night?

Options include nerve-pain medicines a physician prescribes, keeping sheets off your feet with a bed cradle, and good sleep habits. Talk to a clinician about which approach fits you.

Do you offer hyperbaric oxygen for neuropathy?

No. HBOT is not an FDA-cleared indication for neuropathy. We use it only for cleared indications, such as certain diabetic foot ulcers.

Is neuropathy an emergency?

Usually not, but rapidly spreading weakness, trouble breathing, or sudden one-sided numbness needs emergency care. An infected foot sore needs same-day care.
