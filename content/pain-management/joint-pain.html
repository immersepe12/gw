Safety First

Joint Pain That Needs Same-Day Care

Get medical care the same day, or go to urgent care or an emergency room, if you have:

  • A hot, red, swollen joint with fever or chills, which can be a joint infection (septic arthritis) and needs fast treatment
  • A joint that looks deformed, or you cannot use or bear weight on it after an injury
  • Sudden, severe pain and swelling in one joint, especially if you have never had it before
  • Joint pain with a spreading rash, chest pain or trouble breathing

Call 911 for a serious injury or any symptom that feels like an emergency. Our practice is outpatient and does not treat emergencies.

Why Joints Hurt

What Causes Pain in One or Many Joints?

Joint pain is a symptom, not a diagnosis. The pattern of your pain gives important clues.

Osteoarthritis

The most common type of arthritis. It usually affects knees, hips, hands and the spine, causes stiffness that eases within about 30 minutes of moving, and gets worse with heavy use. It tends to develop slowly. Our arthritis pain page covers osteoarthritis care in detail.

Inflammatory arthritis

  • Rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Often affects the same small joints on both sides, such as the fingers and wrists, with morning stiffness lasting an hour or more, swelling and tiredness.
  • Gout. Sudden, intense pain, redness and swelling, often in the big toe, ankle or knee, caused by uric acid crystals. Attacks can start overnight.
  • Psoriatic arthritis and others. May come with skin psoriasis, swollen fingers or toes, or back stiffness.

Injury and overuse

Sprains, tendon problems and bursitis cause pain around a joint, usually tied to a specific activity or event. These often respond well to physical therapy.

Multiple joint pain causes beyond arthritis

Viral infections, thyroid problems, low vitamin D, some medicines, and conditions like lupus or fibromyalgia can cause aches in many joints at once. That is why a careful history matters.

The Evaluation

How a Joint Pain Doctor Works Out the Cause

At a physician visit, expect questions about which joints hurt, how long, what time of day is worst, any swelling or redness, injuries, family history and other symptoms like rashes, fevers or fatigue. Then comes an exam of the painful joints and others you may not have mentioned.

Do you need labs or imaging?

Not always. Testing is chosen to answer a specific question:

  • Blood tests such as inflammation markers, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP antibodies or uric acid when inflammatory arthritis or gout is suspected
  • X-rays for persistent pain, suspected arthritis changes or after an injury
  • Joint fluid testing, done by a specialist, to check for crystals or infection in a swollen joint
  • Ultrasound or MRI in selected cases, usually ordered when results would change the plan

For typical osteoarthritis in an older adult, a diagnosis can often be made from the history and exam alone.

Treatment

Joint Pain Treatment Options

Movement and physical therapy

For most joint pain, staying active is part of the treatment. Physical therapy builds the muscles that support the joint, improves range of motion and teaches you how to pace activity. See our pages on knee pain and hip pain physical therapy.

Weight and activity

For knees and hips, even modest weight loss, when it applies to you, can lower joint load and pain. Low-impact activity such as walking, cycling and water exercise is usually well tolerated.

Non-opioid medicines, with cautions

  • Topical anti-inflammatory gels (such as diclofenac) for knees and hands, with less whole-body exposure than pills
  • Oral anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen or naproxen, which can affect the stomach, kidneys, heart and blood pressure, and interact with blood thinners
  • Acetaminophen, which has a daily limit and must be used carefully with liver disease or alcohol use
  • Condition-specific medicines for gout or inflammatory arthritis, often started or guided by a specialist

Opioids are not recommended as a routine treatment for chronic joint pain. Learn more about non-opioid pain treatment.

Referrals

If tests point to rheumatoid arthritis, gout that keeps coming back, or another inflammatory condition, you'll be referred to a rheumatologist, since early treatment can help protect the joints. For structural problems, injections or joint replacement questions, an orthopedic specialist is the right next step. We do not perform procedures ourselves.

Questions

Joint Pain Treatment: Frequently Asked Questions

What kind of doctor should I see for joint pain?

A primary care or internal medicine physician is a good first stop. They can examine you, order basic tests and start treatment. If the cause looks like rheumatoid arthritis, gout that keeps returning or another inflammatory condition, they refer you to a rheumatologist. For injuries or surgical questions, an orthopedic specialist is the usual next step.

Why do all my joints hurt at once?

Aches in many joints can come from a recent viral illness, inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid problems, low vitamin D, certain medicines, fibromyalgia or widespread osteoarthritis. The pattern, timing and any swelling or stiffness help a physician narrow it down. Pain in many joints with swelling or long morning stiffness deserves a prompt visit.

How can I tell arthritis pain from an injury?

Injury pain usually starts after a specific event, like a twist or fall, and centers on one area. Osteoarthritis builds slowly and feels worse with use. Inflammatory arthritis often brings swelling, warmth and morning stiffness lasting an hour or longer, sometimes in several joints. A physician exam is the best way to tell them apart.

Is ibuprofen safe for joint pain?

For many people, short-term use at the label dose is reasonable. But anti-inflammatory pills can cause stomach bleeding, kidney strain and higher blood pressure, and they interact with blood thinners. Risks rise with age and long-term use. Ask a physician before using them regularly, especially if you have heart, kidney or stomach problems.

When should I worry about a swollen joint?

Get same-day care if a swollen joint is hot and red, you have a fever, the pain is severe and sudden, or you cannot use the joint. These can signal infection or a gout attack. Swelling that lasts more than a few days, keeps returning or appears in several joints should also be checked by a physician soon.
