Safety First

When to Call Your Pediatrician Right Away

Call your child's doctor the same day, or seek emergency care, if your baby or toddler with low tone:

  • Has trouble breathing, breathes very fast, or turns blue or gray around the lips
  • Has trouble sucking, swallowing or feeding, chokes often, or isn't gaining weight
  • Suddenly becomes floppy, very sleepy or hard to wake
  • Loses skills they already had, such as holding up their head, sitting or babbling
  • Has a weak cry, or seizures or unusual movements

Call 911 for breathing problems or if your child is not responding normally. Our practice is outpatient and does not treat emergencies.

The Basics

What Is Hypotonia?

Muscle tone is the amount of tension in a muscle when it is resting. It is not the same as strength, though low tone often makes it harder for a child to build strength and hold positions. Tone is controlled by the brain, nerves and muscles working together.

Hypotonia is a sign, not a diagnosis. It can be mild and benign, which some doctors call benign congenital hypotonia, where a child is a bit loose-jointed and slower with some motor skills but otherwise healthy. It can also be linked to prematurity, genetic conditions such as Down syndrome, or conditions of the brain, nerves or muscles. Only a physician can look for a cause, sometimes with help from a pediatric neurologist or geneticist.

What a "floppy baby" can look like

  • Feels like a rag doll when lifted, or slips through your hands when held under the arms
  • Arms and legs hang straight down instead of staying bent
  • Poor head control past the early months
  • Very flexible joints, such as hips that fall wide open when lying on the back
  • Tires quickly with tummy time or feeding

Older Babies and Toddlers

Hypotonia in Toddlers: Everyday Signs

As children grow, low tone can show up in how they move and play:

  • Leaning on furniture or people, or slumping when sitting
  • Sitting with legs in a W shape or very wide for a stable base (read more about W sitting)
  • Walking with feet wide apart, flat feet, or falling and tiring more than other kids
  • Mouth open at rest, drooling past the toddler years, or messy eating
  • Avoiding climbing, jumping or stairs

Milestones and tone

Children with low tone often reach movement milestones later. The CDC's Learn the Signs. Act Early. program lists skills most children have by certain ages, for example holding the head steady without support by about 4 months, sitting without support by about 9 months, and walking without holding on by about 18 months. A child who has not reached these by those ages should be checked. For a fuller set of movement milestone ranges, see our page on gross motor delays.

Therapy

How Pediatric Physical Therapy Helps Low Muscle Tone

Therapy can't change a child's underlying tone, but it can help a great deal with what tone affects: strength, endurance, balance and reaching milestones. A licensed pediatric physical therapist starts with an evaluation of how your child moves, holds positions, transitions and plays.

Therapy is play-based. Depending on your child's age and needs, it may include:

  • Tummy time and floor play ideas that build head, neck and trunk control
  • Practice with rolling, sitting, crawling, pulling to stand and walking
  • Balance and core activities using balls, benches and obstacle courses
  • Guidance on positioning, carriers, high chairs and, if needed, shoe inserts or supports
  • A home program you can fit into everyday routines

Low tone can also affect hand skills, feeding and speech. Your child may benefit from occupational therapy or a feeding and speech evaluation as well. Progress varies from child to child, and no therapist can promise a timeline.

Questions

Low Muscle Tone: Frequently Asked Questions

Can a baby outgrow low muscle tone?

Some children with mild, benign low tone become stronger and catch up on skills as they grow, though they may stay a bit flexible. When low tone is linked to another condition, it usually continues in some form. Either way, building strength and skills through play and therapy can help your child move with more confidence.

Is low muscle tone a sign of autism?

Some autistic children have low muscle tone, but low tone alone does not mean a child is autistic, and many children with low tone are not. Autism is diagnosed by looking at social communication and behavior. If you have concerns about communication or social skills, talk to your pediatrician and ask about a developmental screening.

What tests are done for hypotonia?

It depends on what your pediatrician finds. Many children with mild low tone need no tests. Others may have blood tests, genetic testing, brain imaging or nerve and muscle studies, often arranged through a pediatric neurologist. The goal is to look for a cause when the history or exam suggests one.

How can I help my floppy baby at home?

Offer plenty of supervised tummy time and floor play, hold your baby in positions that encourage head and trunk control, and limit long stretches in car seats, swings and bouncers. Short, frequent play sessions work better than long ones. A pediatric physical therapist can show you activities matched to your baby's age and needs.

Does my child need a referral for pediatric physical therapy?

For Early Start or a school evaluation, you can contact the Regional Center or school district directly. For private therapy, requirements vary, and many families start by talking with their pediatrician first, which also makes sure any medical causes are looked at.
