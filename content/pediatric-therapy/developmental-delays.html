The Basics

What Does "Gross Motor Delay" Mean?

Gross motor skills are the big movements that use the large muscles of the body: holding up the head, rolling, sitting, crawling, pulling to stand, walking, running and climbing. A gross motor delay means a child reaches these skills later than most children the same age.

A delay is a description, not a diagnosis. Some children are simply on the later side of normal and catch up on their own. Others have an underlying reason, such as low muscle tone, a tight muscle, prematurity, or a medical or neurological condition. Only your child's doctor can sort out the cause. What a physical therapist can do is look closely at how your child moves and help build the skills that are missing.

If your baby was born early, doctors usually use an "adjusted age" for milestones. For example, a baby born two months early who is now 10 months old is often compared with 8-month-olds until about age 2.

Baby Not Walking?

When Do Babies Usually Start Walking?

Most babies take their first independent steps somewhere between about 9 and 15 months, and many walk well by 18 months. That is a wide window, and early or late walking within it doesn't predict how athletic or smart your child will be.

The CDC lists walking without holding on as an 18-month milestone. If your child is not walking by 18 months, talk to your pediatrician. It's also worth asking sooner if:

  • Your baby is not pulling up to stand or cruising along furniture by about 12 months.
  • Your child bears weight on one leg much more than the other or seems to favor one side.
  • Your child's legs seem very stiff, or very floppy, compared with other children.
  • Walking was going well and then got worse.

Some children who walk late also have tiptoe walking or other walking patterns that are worth a look. Our guide to toe walking and torticollis explains those.

Crawling

What If My Baby Doesn't Crawl?

Crawling is no longer on the CDC milestone checklists, because many healthy babies skip it. Some scoot on their bottoms, some roll to get around, and some go straight from sitting to pulling up and walking. What matters more is that your baby finds a way to move and explore, and uses both sides of the body.

Tummy time while your baby is awake and watched helps build the neck, shoulder and trunk strength used for rolling, crawling and sitting. The AAP recommends starting tummy time soon after birth for short periods several times a day, and always placing babies on their backs to sleep.

Next Steps

When to Talk to Your Pediatrician, and Free Options

The CDC's advice is simple: if you are concerned, don't wait. Talk to your child's doctor, share the milestone checklist and ask about a developmental screening. The AAP recommends routine developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months, but you can ask at any visit.

Call the pediatrician promptly if your child:

  • Loses a movement skill they used to have.
  • Has new weakness, trouble using an arm or leg, or a sudden change in walking.
  • Has a delay along with feeding problems, poor weight gain or seizures.

Free evaluations you can request yourself

Under 3: California Early Start offers free developmental evaluations, and services for eligible children from birth to 36 months. In Stanislaus County, contact Valley Mountain Regional Center. A doctor's referral is not required.

Age 3 and up: Your public school district must evaluate children who may need special education, at no cost, under the federal IDEA law. Put your request in writing.

These programs are a good, honest first step. Some families also choose clinic therapy alongside them or while they wait.

Pediatric PT

How Pediatric Physical Therapy Helps With Motor Delays

At our Modesto practice, a licensed physical therapist starts with an evaluation. They watch your child play and move, check muscle tone, strength, flexibility and balance, and may use a standardized motor test to compare skills with children the same age. You'll get a plain-language explanation of what they see.

Therapy itself looks like play. Depending on your child, it may include:

  • Strength building for the neck, trunk, hips and legs through reaching, rolling and climbing games.
  • Balance practice, such as standing at a low table, stepping over cushions or walking on different surfaces.
  • Transitions, like getting from lying to sitting or sitting to standing, which are often the hardest part.
  • Parent coaching, so the short home activities fit into your normal day.
  • Coordination with your child's other care, including speech or occupational therapy within our practice and your pediatrician, with your permission.

Movement delays sometimes show up alongside delays in talking or hand skills. If you've noticed those too, read about speech delay in toddlers or see occupational therapy for kids. Every child is different, so no one can promise when a skill will arrive. What we can do is give you a clear picture, a practical plan and regular check-ins.

Questions

Motor Delays: Frequently Asked Questions

When should I worry if my baby is not walking?

Most children walk on their own by 18 months, which is the CDC milestone. If your child isn't walking by then, or isn't pulling to stand by about 12 months, talk to your pediatrician. Most babies take their first steps between about 9 and 15 months. Early or late walking within that window doesn't predict how athletic or smart your child will be.

Is it a problem if my baby never crawled?

Not on its own. Many healthy babies skip crawling, and it is no longer a CDC milestone. Ask your doctor if your baby isn't moving around in some way or uses one side much more than the other. Milestones happen across a range of ages, so one skipped skill is not a diagnosis. Your child's doctor can help sort out whether anything needs a closer look.

Will my child grow out of a motor delay?

Some children catch up on their own and some need support. Waiting to see can delay help, so the CDC advises acting on concerns early with your pediatrician. A delay is a description, not a diagnosis. Some children are simply on the later side of normal, while others have an underlying reason, such as low muscle tone or prematurity.

Can I get a free evaluation for my toddler?

Yes. California Early Start offers free evaluations for children under 3 through regional centers, and school districts evaluate children 3 and older at no cost. A good first step is a conversation with your pediatrician. Many families use these programs alongside private therapy.

What does a physical therapist do for a baby?

They use guided play, positioning and gentle exercises to build strength and balance, and teach parents short activities to do at home between visits. A physical therapist looks closely at how your child moves and helps build the skills that are missing. Only your child's doctor can sort out the cause of a delay.
