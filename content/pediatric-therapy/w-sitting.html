The Basics

What Is W Sitting and Why Do Kids Do It?

Many toddlers fall into W sitting naturally, often moving into it from crawling or kneeling. Young children have flexible hips, and this position gives them a wide, steady base. That frees their hands for play without needing much trunk balance. It is especially common between about ages 2 and 6, and many children grow out of it as their core strength and balance improve.

Online you may read strong warnings that W sitting causes hip problems or delays. The evidence for that is limited. In a healthy, typically developing child who also sits in other ways, occasional W sitting is usually just a comfortable choice, not a problem to fix.

Worth a Closer Look

Is W Sitting Bad? When It May Be a Sign

W sitting itself is rarely the issue. It is more useful as a clue. Talk with your pediatrician or a pediatric physical therapist if your child:

  • Always W sits and seems to have no other comfortable way to sit
  • Can't sit other ways, such as cross-legged, side-sitting or with legs straight out, or topples when they try
  • Shows signs of low muscle tone, such as feeling floppy, slumping, tiring quickly or being very loose-jointed
  • Has hip concerns, such as hip pain, a limp, clicking, a history of hip dysplasia, or legs that turn in a lot when walking
  • Uses one side much more, sits lopsided with one leg always out, or seems stiff or tight in the legs
  • Is behind on movement milestones, or also walks on tiptoe most of the time (see toe walking)

In these cases, W sitting may be how a child makes up for weakness, tightness or a joint shape that needs a look. Some children with in-toeing sit this way because their thigh bones are naturally rotated inward, which usually improves with age but is worth having checked.

Get prompt medical care for any sudden limp, refusal to walk, or a hot, swollen joint, especially with fever.

At Home

Gentle Alternatives to W Sitting

If your child is otherwise healthy, you don't need to correct every W sit. For a child who sits this way a lot, it can help to offer variety. Keep it light and playful, not a source of nagging.

  • Offer other floor positions. Try "criss-cross applesauce," side-sitting with both legs to one side (switching sides), or long sitting with legs straight out.
  • Give a low seat. A small stool, cube chair or sitting on a parent's lap during play.
  • Play at a table or on the tummy. Standing at a low table or lying on the belly for puzzles and books both build strength.
  • Use a simple cue. A friendly "fix your feet" or "crisscross, please" works better than a lecture.
  • Build core and hip strength through play. Climbing playground ladders, wheelbarrow walking, crab walks, ball games and riding a tricycle.

Expect your child to slip back into W sitting sometimes. That is normal. The goal is a child who can sit and move in many ways.

Next Steps

When to Ask Your Pediatrician or a Physical Therapist

If any of the signs above apply, start with your pediatrician. They can check your child's hips, legs and development, and decide whether an evaluation is needed. A licensed pediatric physical therapist can then look at strength, flexibility, balance and how your child moves, and build a play-based plan if one is needed.

Free options

Questions

W Sitting: Frequently Asked Questions

Is W sitting bad for my toddler's hips?

For most healthy toddlers, occasional W sitting is not known to harm the hips. Strong claims that it causes hip problems aren't well supported by research. It is worth a check if your child has known hip dysplasia, hip pain, a limp or clicking, or if they can only sit in a W. Your pediatrician can examine the hips.

At what age should a child stop W sitting?

There is no set age. Many children W sit less as they get older, usually by early school age, as core strength and balance improve. If your child still always W sits, can't sit other ways, or W sitting comes with other concerns like frequent falls or delays, ask your pediatrician about an evaluation.

Should I stop my child every time they W sit?

Usually not. Constant correcting can turn play into a struggle. If your child sits in many ways, there's no need. If they W sit most of the time, offer other positions and seats, use a friendly cue now and then, and add strength-building play. A physical therapist can suggest ideas suited to your child.

Can W sitting cause pigeon toes?

W sitting is more often linked with in-toeing than a cause of it. Many children who are naturally pigeon-toed, because their thigh bones turn inward, find W sitting comfortable. In-toeing often improves on its own with age. If it is severe, one-sided, causes frequent tripping or pain, or isn't improving, have it checked.

Can a physical therapist help with W sitting?

Yes, when W sitting is tied to weakness, low tone, tightness or delayed skills. A pediatric physical therapist evaluates your child, then uses play to build core and hip strength, balance and new ways to sit and move. For a typically developing child who also sits in other ways, therapy usually isn't needed.
