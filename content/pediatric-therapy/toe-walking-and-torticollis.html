Toe Walking

Toe Walking in Toddlers and Children

Many toddlers walk on their toes when they are first learning to walk. For most, it fades as balance improves, often by around age 2. When a child keeps walking on tiptoes most of the time after that, doctors call it persistent toe walking.

Why some children keep toe walking

Often no cause is found. This is called idiopathic toe walking, and it can run in families. In other children, toe walking is linked to tight calf muscles or Achilles tendons, or to a neurological, muscular or developmental condition. That's why your pediatrician should check, rather than a therapist alone. A physical therapist can describe how your child walks, but the doctor looks for the reason.

When to talk to your pediatrician

  • Your child still walks on tiptoes most of the time after about age 2 to 3.
  • Your child cannot stand flat-footed, or the heels won't come down when you gently try.
  • Toe walking is on one side only.
  • Your child walked normally before and then started toe walking.
  • It comes with stiffness, weakness, frequent falls, or delays in talking or other skills.

How toe walking is usually managed

Care depends on the cause and how flexible the ankle is. Common first steps include calf stretching, strengthening, balance work and walking practice. Some children also need night splints or serial casting to gently stretch the calf over time, and a small number need an orthopedic opinion. Those decisions are made with your child's physician.

Torticollis

Torticollis and Flat Head in Babies

Congenital muscular torticollis happens when the sternocleidomastoid, a muscle on the side of the neck, is short or tight. The baby's head tilts toward one shoulder and the chin turns the other way. It may be linked to the baby's position in the womb or during birth. Some babies also have a small, soft lump in the neck muscle in the first weeks.

Signs parents notice

  • Your baby always looks or sleeps with the head turned to the same side.
  • Your baby has trouble turning to look at you on one side.
  • Feeding is easier on one breast or one side.
  • A flat spot develops on one side or the back of the head.

Torticollis and flat head (plagiocephaly)

A baby's skull is soft. When the head always rests in the same position, a flat spot can form. This is called positional plagiocephaly, and it often goes along with torticollis. Back sleeping is still the safest way for babies to sleep, as the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends. Supervised tummy time while awake, changing the direction your baby faces in the crib, and holding your baby in different positions all help take pressure off the flat spot.

Whether a baby needs a helmet is a decision for your pediatrician or a specialist, usually based on how severe the flat spot is and how your baby responds to positioning and therapy.

Why early matters

The 2018 clinical practice guideline on torticollis from the American Physical Therapy Association's Academy of Pediatric Physical Therapy recommends referral to physical therapy as soon as torticollis is identified. Babies who start earlier tend to need a shorter course of care than babies who start later. If you notice a constant head tilt, ask your pediatrician at your next visit or sooner.

Pediatric PT

How Physical Therapy Helps

At our Modesto practice, a licensed physical therapist begins with an evaluation: neck or ankle range of motion, strength on both sides, how your child moves and plays, and your questions. For babies with torticollis, therapy usually includes:

  • Gentle neck stretches that you learn to do safely at home.
  • Strengthening through play, encouraging your baby to turn toward the harder side for toys and faces.
  • Positioning and carrying tips for feeding, play and car seats.
  • Tummy time ideas that work for babies who dislike it.

For toe walking, therapy focuses on calf flexibility, strength, balance and walking practice, often with games like walking on heels, squatting to pick up toys or climbing ramps. The therapist shares progress with your pediatrician, with your permission, and tells you honestly if a referral to another specialist seems wise.

Questions

Toe Walking and Torticollis: Frequently Asked Questions

Is toe walking normal for a toddler?

It's common in new walkers and often fades by about age 2. If it continues most of the time after age 2 to 3, or only on one side, talk to your pediatrician.

Is toe walking a sign of autism?

Toe walking alone does not mean a child is autistic. It is seen more often in some developmental conditions, so your pediatrician may ask about other skills. Only a qualified professional can make a diagnosis.

Can torticollis go away on its own?

Mild cases sometimes improve with positioning, but guidelines recommend physical therapy as soon as torticollis is found, because earlier care tends to mean a shorter course.

Does my baby need a helmet for a flat head?

Many flat spots improve with positioning and tummy time. Your pediatrician or a specialist decides whether a helmet is needed based on severity and progress.
