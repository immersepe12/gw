California Law

Do You Need a Referral for Physical Therapy in California?

No, not to begin. California is a "direct access" state for physical therapy. Under Business and Professions Code section 2620.1, you can book an evaluation with a licensed physical therapist without first seeing a doctor. The therapist can examine you, make a plan and start treatment.

The law has guardrails. Summarized in plain words, as of 2026:

  • Time and visit limit. Without a physician's sign-off, a physical therapist can treat you for up to 45 calendar days or 12 visits, whichever comes first. The clock starts with your first treatment.
  • Notice to you. The physical therapist must tell you, in writing, that they are not a physician and that some conditions need a physician's diagnosis.
  • Referral when needed. If the therapist sees signs of a condition outside their scope, they must refer you to an appropriate provider.
  • Limits on scope. Without a physician, a physical therapist cannot make a medical diagnosis of disease, and some services need a doctor's order.

The 45-day, 12-visit structure came from Senate Bill 1438, which took effect on January 1, 2023. Laws can change, so if timing matters for you, check the current text on the California Legislature's site (linked in the sources below) or ask the Physical Therapy Board of California.

Why direct access matters

Before direct access, many people waited weeks for a doctor's appointment just to get a PT referral for a simple strain or a stiff shoulder. Starting sooner means you get advice and exercises while the problem is fresh. It also removes a visit, and a cost, for problems that clearly fit physical therapy.

The Next Step

What Happens After 45 Days or 12 Visits

Many people finish physical therapy within the direct-access window. If you need more care, the law requires a physician and surgeon (or a podiatrist, for problems within podiatry's scope) to examine you and sign and date your plan of care. The exam can be in person or by telehealth.

That sign-off is not just paperwork. It is a check that your recovery is on track and that nothing else needs medical attention. After the plan is signed, your therapy can continue as the plan describes.

Where people get stuck

The usual hold-up is scheduling. If your physical therapist works at a separate clinic, you may need to book a new appointment with your doctor, send records across, and wait for the signed plan to come back. Your therapy can pause while that happens.

A Common Catch

Health Plans Can Have Their Own Referral Rules

State law sets what a physical therapist is allowed to do. It does not control what a health plan agrees to pay for. Some plans, especially HMOs and some Medicare and Medi-Cal plans, require a referral or prior authorization before they cover PT, even though the law lets you start without one.

If you plan to use coverage anywhere, call the member services number on your card and ask:

  • Do I need a referral or prior authorization for outpatient physical therapy?
  • Is there a visit limit per year?
  • Does the clinic I'm considering need to be in my network?

Safety First

When You Should See a Doctor Before Physical Therapy

Direct access is right for most aches, strains and stiffness. It is not the right first step for everything. Go to the emergency room or call 911 for:

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin or inner thighs
  • Sudden weakness, numbness on one side, trouble speaking or a drooping face
  • Pain after a serious fall, crash or other major injury
  • Back pain with fever, or chest pain
  • A calf that is suddenly swollen, red, warm and painful

See a doctor first, soon, if you have pain that wakes you every night, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer with new pain, or a joint you cannot put weight on after an injury. After surgery, follow your surgeon's instructions; your surgeon usually sets the rehab protocol, and you can read more in our post-surgical rehab guide.

Questions

Direct Access: Frequently Asked Questions

Can I see a physical therapist without a referral in California?

Yes. Under California law, as of 2026, you can be evaluated and treated by a licensed physical therapist without a referral, within the 45-day or 12-visit limit. The therapist can examine you, make a plan and start treatment. California is a "direct access" state, so you can book an evaluation without first seeing a doctor.

What is the 45-day, 12-visit rule?

A physical therapist may treat you without a physician's signature for up to 45 calendar days or 12 visits, whichever comes first. After that, a physician must examine you and sign your plan of care. The clock starts with your first treatment. The physician exam can be in person or by telehealth.

Does my insurance still need a referral?

It might. Health plans can require referrals or prior authorization to pay for PT regardless of state law. Check with your plan before you start. Direct access is about what state law allows. Your plan's payment rules are separate, so a quick call to your plan can save surprises later.

Can a physical therapist diagnose my condition?

A physical therapist can assess movement problems and make a physical therapy diagnosis, but cannot diagnose disease. If something needs a medical diagnosis, they refer you to a physician. The law also requires the therapist to tell you in writing that they are not a physician and that some conditions need a physician's diagnosis.

Who signs my plan of care after 12 visits?

A physician and surgeon, or a podiatrist for foot and ankle problems within their scope. At our practice, that review can happen with our own physician. That sign-off is not just paperwork. It is a check that your recovery is on track and that nothing else needs medical attention.
