Safety First

Achilles Rupture Warning Signs

Achilles tendinopathy builds up over weeks or months. A rupture is different, and it needs urgent care. Get seen the same day at urgent care or an emergency room if:

  • You felt a sudden pop or snap at the back of the ankle, or felt like someone kicked you there
  • You cannot push off, rise onto your toes on that side, or walk normally afterward
  • There is a gap or dent you can feel in the tendon, with sudden swelling or bruising

A partial or complete tear can be missed if you wait. Early treatment, whether with a boot or surgery, is decided by a physician. Also see a doctor first if the calf is hot, swollen and tender, which can signal a blood clot, or if heel pain comes with fever.

The Basics

What Is Achilles Tendinopathy?

The Achilles tendon joins your calf muscles to your heel bone. It handles huge forces every time you walk, climb stairs, run or jump. When the load on the tendon goes up faster than it can adapt, it can become painful and thickened. Researchers now see this mostly as a problem of tendon overload and poor repair, not ongoing inflammation, which is why the term tendinopathy has replaced tendinitis.

Two common types

  • Mid-portion. Pain about two to six centimeters above the heel, often with a tender, thicker area in the cord.
  • Insertional. Pain right where the tendon attaches to the back of the heel. It is often worse with shoes that press there and with deep ankle bends.

Typical signs of heel cord pain

  • Stiffness and pain with the first steps in the morning or after sitting, which eases as you warm up
  • Pain at the start of a run that may settle, then return worse later or the next day
  • Tenderness when you squeeze the tendon

Common triggers

A sudden jump in running mileage, hills or speed work; new shoes or going barefoot more; long shifts on hard floors; weak calves; and getting back into sport after time off. Age, extra body weight, diabetes and some medicines raise the risk too.

Medication Note

Fluoroquinolone Antibiotics and the Achilles

Fluoroquinolone antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, carry an FDA boxed warning for tendinitis and tendon rupture, most often in the Achilles. The risk is higher in people over 60, people taking corticosteroids, and people who have had a kidney, heart or lung transplant. Tendon problems can start within hours of a dose or even months after finishing.

If you notice new heel cord pain while taking or after taking one of these antibiotics, contact the prescriber right away. Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own. Tell your physical therapist about any recent antibiotics, too, because it changes how carefully loading is started.

Exercise Therapy

Achilles Tendinopathy Exercises That Load the Tendon

The 2018 clinical practice guideline from the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy, published in the Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy (JOSPT), recommends resistance exercise that loads the tendon as a core treatment. Tendons respond to load. Rest alone tends to make them weaker, and pain often returns when activity resumes.

Eccentric and heavy slow resistance

Two well-studied approaches are:

  • Eccentric heel drops. You rise onto your toes with both feet, then slowly lower on the sore side, often off the edge of a step.
  • Heavy slow resistance. Slow, controlled calf raises with gradually increasing weight, such as on a leg press or with a backpack, done a few times a week.

Both have research support. Your therapist picks a starting point that suits your pain and goals, then adds load over time. For insertional pain, exercises are often done on flat ground instead of dropping below the step, because deep ankle bends can press on the attachment.

How much pain is okay?

Many programs use a simple pain-monitoring rule: discomfort during exercise up to about 3 to 5 out of 10 can be acceptable if it settles within 24 hours and morning stiffness doesn't get worse. Your therapist will set limits for you.

Other parts of care

  • Strengthening the hips and the rest of the leg
  • A graded return to running, jumping or long walks
  • Short-term heel lifts for insertional pain, when they help
  • Education about footwear and load at work

Tendons adapt slowly. Many people need consistent exercise for several months, and progress can't be promised on a set timeline. Pain in the arch instead of the heel cord may be something else; see our page on plantar fasciitis physical therapy.

Questions

Achilles Tendinitis Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

How long does Achilles tendinitis take to heal?

It varies widely. Tendons adapt slowly, so many people need two to three months or more of steady loading exercise, and long-standing cases can take longer. Pain often improves before full strength returns. Your physical therapist tracks your morning stiffness, calf strength and activity level so you can see progress and adjust the plan.

Should I stop running with Achilles pain?

Not always. Many runners can keep running at a reduced distance or pace if pain stays mild and settles by the next morning. Hills, speed work and jumping usually need to be cut back first. Stop and get checked if pain is sharp, getting worse week to week, or you felt a pop.

Is stretching enough for Achilles tendinopathy?

Stretching alone is usually not enough. Research and the 2018 JOSPT guideline point to resistance exercise that loads the tendon, such as eccentric heel drops or heavy slow calf raises. Gentle calf stretching may feel good for some people, but for insertional pain deep stretches can press on the sore spot.

Do I need an MRI for heel cord pain?

Usually not. A clinician can often identify Achilles tendinopathy from your history and an exam. Imaging such as ultrasound or MRI is considered when a tear is suspected, the diagnosis is unclear, or pain doesn't improve with a good trial of care. A physician decides whether imaging is needed.

Can I see a physical therapist for Achilles pain without a referral?

Yes. Under California law, as of 2026, you can see a physical therapist without a referral for up to 45 calendar days or 12 visits, whichever comes first. After that, a physician needs to sign the plan of care. Having a physician in our practice makes that step simpler. Rules can change.
