Safety First

Back Pain Warning Signs That Need a Doctor First

Most back pain is not dangerous, even when it hurts a lot. A small number of cases need urgent medical care. Call 911 or go to an emergency room if back pain comes with:

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or new trouble starting to urinate
  • Numbness in the groin, buttocks or inner thighs ("saddle" numbness)
  • Sudden or quickly worsening weakness in one or both legs
  • Fever, or pain after a serious fall, crash or other major trauma

See a doctor before starting PT if you have back pain with unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, pain that is worse at night and doesn't ease with rest or position changes, or long-term steroid use. Our physician can evaluate you first if needed.

The Evidence

Why Physical Therapy Is Recommended for Lower Back Pain

Low back pain is very common. Many adults have it at some point, and most short-term episodes improve within a few weeks. For some people, though, it lingers or keeps coming back.

Major guidelines favor active, non-drug approaches first. The American College of Physicians' 2017 guideline on low back pain recommends that most people start with non-drug treatments such as exercise, spinal manipulation, heat, and mind-body approaches before medication, and that people with chronic back pain use exercise-based therapy as a first-line option. The CDC's 2022 opioid prescribing guideline also emphasizes non-opioid options for back pain.

Physical therapy fits that advice. It is built around exercise and education, and it puts you in charge of your recovery. It does not promise that pain disappears, but research reviews show exercise therapy can modestly reduce pain and improve function in chronic low back pain, and staying active tends to help more than bed rest.

What "better" usually looks like

Progress in back pain is often measured by what you can do: sitting through a work shift, lifting a child, sleeping through the night, getting back to the gym or the garden. Pain often eases along the way, but function is the goal your therapist tracks most closely.

Understanding Your Back

Common Causes of Lower Back Pain

In many cases, no single structure can be blamed. Doctors often call this "nonspecific" low back pain. That is not a brush-off: it means the pain is real but isn't caused by a serious disease, and it usually responds to movement-based care. Common contributors include:

  • Muscle and ligament strain from lifting, twisting or a sudden movement
  • Disc problems, including bulging or herniated discs
  • Age-related changes such as disc degeneration or arthritis in the spine's joints; these are common on imaging even in people without pain
  • Spinal stenosis, a narrowing that can cause leg pain or heaviness when walking or standing
  • Work and life load: long hours sitting or driving, heavy farm, warehouse or construction work, poor sleep and stress

Imaging such as an X-ray or MRI is usually not needed in the first weeks unless there are warning signs. Your clinician decides whether you need it.

Your Plan

What Physical Therapy for Back Pain Involves

The evaluation

Your therapist asks how the pain started and what changes it. They test how your spine and hips move, check strength and nerve function in your legs, and look at how you bend, lift, sit and walk. Some people feel better bending forward, others bending back; finding your pattern helps choose exercises.

Common parts of treatment

  • Exercise. Movements matched to your pattern, then strengthening for the trunk and hips and general conditioning such as walking.
  • Manual therapy. Hands-on joint and soft-tissue techniques, used when they help you move more comfortably.
  • Pain education. Understanding how pain works can reduce fear of movement, which often keeps people stuck.
  • Movement practice. Safer ways to lift, bend, get in and out of a car, and return to your job or sport.
  • Pacing. Building activity gradually so you avoid the "boom and bust" cycle of overdoing it on good days.

How long it takes

There is no set number of visits. Recent back pain often needs fewer visits than pain that has lasted months. Your therapist reviews progress regularly and changes the plan if you are not improving, including checking in with our physician.

Disc and Nerve Pain

Herniated Discs, Sciatica and Physical Therapy

A herniated disc happens when some of the soft center of a spinal disc pushes out through its tougher outer layer. It can irritate a nearby nerve and cause pain, tingling or numbness down the leg. Pain that travels down the back of the leg is often called sciatica.

Many herniated discs improve over weeks to months without surgery. Physical therapy for a herniated disc commonly includes:

  • Finding positions and movements that ease leg symptoms, sometimes using repeated movements
  • Gentle nerve mobility exercises when appropriate
  • Gradual strengthening and walking
  • Tips for sitting, sleeping and lifting while symptoms settle

A good sign during treatment is "centralization": leg pain retreating toward the back, even if back pain lingers for a while. Your therapist watches for this. Increasing leg weakness or numbness means you need a physician's evaluation.

Between Visits

What You Can Do at Home for Back Pain

  • Keep moving gently. Short walks and normal daily activity, within comfort, usually help more than bed rest.
  • Try heat. A heating pad or warm shower can ease muscle tension for many people.
  • Change positions often. If you sit or drive for long periods, get up every 30 to 45 minutes.
  • Do your home program. A few minutes most days matters more than an occasional long session.
  • Protect your sleep. Poor sleep and pain feed each other. Pillows under or between the knees can help you find a comfortable position.

Ask a clinician before starting new medicines, including over-the-counter ones, especially if you have kidney, stomach or heart problems or take blood thinners.

Questions

Back Pain Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Is physical therapy good for lower back pain?

For most people, yes. Clinical guidelines recommend exercise-based, non-drug treatment as a first step for low back pain, and physical therapy is built around that.

How many PT sessions are needed for back pain?

It varies with how long you've had pain, your goals and how consistently you do home exercises. Your therapist sets a plan after the evaluation and reviews it regularly.

Can physical therapy help a herniated disc?

Many herniated discs improve without surgery, and PT can help you manage symptoms and regain movement. Worsening leg weakness or bladder changes need urgent medical care.

Should I rest or stay active with back pain?

Staying gently active usually helps more than bed rest. Avoid what clearly makes symptoms much worse, and build activity back gradually.

Do I need an MRI before physical therapy?

Usually not. Imaging is generally reserved for warning signs or symptoms that don't improve. A clinician decides whether you need it.
