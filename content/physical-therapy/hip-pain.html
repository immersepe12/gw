Safety First

Hip Symptoms That Need a Doctor First

Most hip pain is safe to bring to a physical therapist. Some signs need medical care first:

  • An older adult who falls and then cannot stand or bear weight, or whose leg looks shorter or turned outward. This can be a hip fracture. Call 911 or go to the emergency room.
  • A hot, painful hip with fever or chills, which can signal infection
  • Hip or groin pain with a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, or long-term steroid use
  • Deep groin pain that gets steadily worse and makes you limp, especially if you drink heavily or take steroids
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels

Pain felt in the hip can also come from the lower back. Our physician can evaluate you before therapy if anything is unclear.

Outer Hip Pain

Hip Bursitis and Greater Trochanteric Pain

Pain on the outside of the hip used to be called hip bursitis. Today it is often called greater trochanteric pain syndrome, because the main problem is usually irritation of the tendons of the gluteal (buttock) muscles where they attach to the bony point of the hip. It is common in women over 40 and in people who are increasing their walking or running.

Typical signs are pain lying on that side at night, pain climbing stairs, and soreness after standing on one leg or sitting with your legs crossed. Research has found that education plus a progressive exercise program helps many people more than waiting it out. Physical therapy usually includes:

  • Strengthening the gluteal muscles, starting gently and building up
  • Avoiding positions that squeeze the tendons, such as crossing your legs or hanging on one hip while standing
  • Sleeping tips, like a pillow between your knees
  • A gradual plan to return to walking, stairs or running

Osteoarthritis

Physical Therapy for Hip Arthritis

Hip osteoarthritis often causes groin or front-of-thigh pain, stiffness in the morning or after sitting, and trouble putting on socks or getting in and out of a car. Exercise is strongly recommended for hip osteoarthritis in major guidelines, including the American College of Rheumatology guideline.

Physical therapy for hip arthritis focuses on strength in the hip and thigh, range of motion, walking tolerance and practical changes such as a cane in the opposite hand on bad days. Exercise does not rebuild cartilage, but many people move and feel better as strength and confidence improve. If you are heading toward a hip replacement, see our post-surgery rehab page, and for a physician's view on medicines, see arthritis pain care.

Other Causes

Hip Flexor Strains, Impingement and More

  • Hip flexor strain. A pull in the muscles at the front of the hip, often from sprinting or kicking. Rest from the aggravating activity for a short time, then gradual strengthening, usually helps.
  • Hip impingement (FAI). Bone shape at the hip joint can pinch tissue with deep bending, causing groin pain with squatting or long sitting. Exercise-based care is a common first step; some people later need a surgeon's opinion.
  • Labral irritation. The ring of cartilage around the hip socket can cause clicking or catching. Many people improve with strengthening and movement changes.
  • Referred pain from the back. Buttock pain that travels down the leg may come from the spine. See back pain physical therapy or sciatica care.

Your Plan

What Physical Therapy for Hip Pain Involves

The evaluation

Your therapist asks where the pain is, what brings it on and how it affects sleep and daily tasks. They check hip motion, strength, balance and how you walk, climb a step and stand on one leg. They also screen your back and knee, since nearby joints often share the load.

Hip pain exercises your therapist may use

  • Glute bridges to strengthen the back of the hip
  • Side-lying or standing hip abduction for the muscles on the side of the hip
  • Sit-to-stand practice from a chair, building toward fewer hand pushes
  • Step-ups and balance work to improve control on one leg
  • Gentle range-of-motion work for stiffness, when it suits your condition

Which exercises fit, and how hard, depends on your diagnosis. Stretches that help arthritis can irritate tendon pain on the side of the hip, which is one reason a proper assessment matters.

Tips at home

  • Keep walking at a comfortable pace and distance rather than resting completely.
  • Break up long sitting, and use a firm, higher chair if low seats hurt.
  • Ask a clinician before starting new pain medicines if you have kidney, stomach or heart problems or take blood thinners.

Mild soreness that settles by the next day is common. Progress timelines vary and can't be promised.

Questions

Hip Pain Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Can physical therapy help hip bursitis?

Often, yes. Most pain on the side of the hip comes from irritated gluteal tendons, and research supports education plus progressive strengthening for this problem. Your therapist also helps you avoid positions that squeeze the tendons, such as crossing your legs. Improvement usually takes several weeks of steady exercise, and timelines vary from person to person.

What are good exercises for hip pain?

Common hip pain exercises include glute bridges, side-lying leg lifts, sit-to-stand practice, step-ups and balance work. The right choice depends on the cause. Stretches that help a stiff arthritic hip can aggravate tendon pain on the side of the hip. A physical therapist can match exercises and difficulty to your diagnosis and progress them safely.

When is hip pain an emergency?

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if an older adult falls and cannot stand or put weight on the leg, or the leg looks shorter or turned outward. These can be signs of a hip fracture. A hot, painful hip with fever also needs same-day medical care, since it can signal infection.

Is walking good for hip arthritis?

For most people, yes. Regular walking at a comfortable pace helps keep the hip moving and the muscles strong. Start with shorter walks and build gradually. A cane held in the opposite hand can ease load on painful days. Your physical therapist can help you set a walking plan that fits your hip.

Do I need a referral for hip physical therapy?

Not to start. California's direct access law lets you see a physical therapist without a referral for up to 45 days or 12 visits, whichever comes first. After that, a physician or other qualified provider needs to sign your plan. Some insurance plans have their own referral rules, so check with your plan.
