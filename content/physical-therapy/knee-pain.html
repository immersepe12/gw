Safety First

Knee Symptoms That Need a Doctor First

Most knee pain is safe to bring to a physical therapist. Get urgent medical care if you have:

  • A knee that looks deformed, or you cannot put any weight on it after an injury
  • A hot, red, swollen knee with fever, which can signal infection
  • A swollen, painful, warm calf, which can signal a blood clot
  • A loud pop with rapid swelling after a twist or fall

A knee that locks and won't straighten, or keeps giving way, should be checked by a physician too. Our physician can evaluate you before therapy if needed.

Front-of-Knee Pain

Runner's Knee (Patellofemoral Pain)

Runner's knee, or patellofemoral pain, is an ache around or behind the kneecap. You don't have to be a runner to get it. It often flares with stairs, squatting, kneeling, or sitting for a long time with bent knees (sometimes called the "moviegoer's sign"). It is common in teens, active adults and people who have recently increased their activity.

Exercise therapy is the main treatment. Research reviews support combining hip and knee strengthening, which tends to work better than knee exercises alone. Physical therapy for runner's knee usually includes:

  • Strengthening the thigh (quadriceps) and hip muscles
  • Adjusting training load, such as running distance, hills or stair work, for a while rather than stopping everything
  • Practicing squats, steps and landing with better control
  • Short-term tools such as taping, when they help you stay active

Improvement often takes several weeks of consistent exercise. Your therapist helps you build back to running or sport step by step.

Osteoarthritis

Physical Therapy for Knee Arthritis

Knee osteoarthritis is a wearing and remodeling of the joint over time. It causes pain, stiffness after rest, and sometimes swelling. It is one of the most common reasons older adults lose the ability to walk distances or climb stairs comfortably.

Exercise is strongly recommended in major guidelines, including the 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation guideline, for people with knee osteoarthritis. The CDC also recommends physical activity as a proven way to reduce arthritis pain and improve function. Physical therapy helps you do that safely:

  • Strength training for the thigh and hip muscles that support the knee
  • Range-of-motion work to ease stiffness
  • Low-impact conditioning such as walking programs or cycling
  • Practical tips on pacing, footwear and, when useful, a cane or brace

Exercise does not regrow cartilage, and arthritis changes on an X-ray don't always match how much it hurts. Many people with arthritis on imaging function well once their strength and confidence improve. If you are considering a knee replacement, a period of PT is often recommended first, and stronger muscles before surgery may help recovery after it. Other Causes

Other Common Causes of Knee Pain

  • Patellar tendinopathy (jumper's knee). Pain just below the kneecap with jumping or squatting. Gradually loaded tendon exercises are central to treatment.
  • IT band syndrome. Pain on the outside of the knee, often in runners and cyclists. Load changes and hip strengthening are common parts of care.
  • Meniscus tears. Many degenerative meniscus tears in middle-aged and older adults respond well to exercise-based care. Some tears, especially in younger people after injury, need a surgeon's opinion.
  • Ligament sprains. MCL sprains often recover with rehab. ACL tears need a surgeon's evaluation; see our sports injury guide.

Your Plan

What Knee Pain Physical Therapy Involves

The evaluation

Your therapist asks when and how the pain started and which activities bring it on. They check swelling, knee bend and straightening, strength in the thigh and hip, balance, and how you walk, squat and step. They also look above and below the knee, because hip weakness and foot mechanics can add load to the knee.

Treatment

  • A strengthening program that starts where you are and progresses each week
  • Movement practice for stairs, getting up from a chair, kneeling or running
  • Hands-on techniques to ease stiffness when helpful
  • A plan for activity: what to keep doing, what to adjust for now
  • A home program you can keep using after discharge

What you can do at home

  • Stay active within comfort. Walking, cycling and swimming are usually kinder to sore knees than running or jumping.
  • Break up long sitting. Stand and bend your knees gently every 30 to 45 minutes.
  • Use stairs wisely. While pain settles, lead with the stronger leg going up and the sore leg going down, and use the handrail.
  • Mind your weight load. Each pound of body weight adds force through the knee with every step, so gradual weight changes, if they apply to you, can ease strain.
  • Keep up your exercises. Short, regular sessions most days build strength more reliably than occasional long ones.

Ask a clinician before starting new pain medicines, including over-the-counter ones, if you have kidney, stomach or heart problems or take blood thinners.

Mild soreness after exercise is common. A useful rule many therapists use: pain during exercise that stays mild and settles by the next day is usually acceptable. Your therapist will give you clear limits for your situation. Progress timelines vary and can't be promised.

Questions

Knee Pain Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Can physical therapy help knee arthritis?

Yes, for many people. Exercise is strongly recommended for knee osteoarthritis, and PT helps you strengthen and move with less pain. It does not rebuild cartilage.

How long does runner's knee take to get better?

It varies. Many people notice progress over several weeks of consistent strengthening and load changes, but some take longer. Your therapist tracks progress with you.

Should I keep walking if my knee hurts?

Often yes, at a level that keeps pain mild. Total rest can weaken the muscles that protect the knee. Ask your therapist for limits that fit your condition.

Do I need an X-ray or MRI before knee PT?

Not always. Imaging is usually reserved for significant injuries, warning signs or pain that doesn't improve. A clinician decides whether you need it.

Can physical therapy make knee pain worse?

It can feel sore at first, but a well-planned program should not make your knee worse over time. Your physical therapist starts with what your knee tolerates and builds up gradually. Mild soreness that settles within a day is common. Sharp pain, new swelling, locking or the knee giving way are signs to tell your therapist so they can change the plan or refer you to a physician.
