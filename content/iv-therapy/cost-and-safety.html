Cost

What Affects the Cost of IV Therapy

Prices for IV drips vary widely from place to place. A few things drive most of the difference:

  • Who screens and orders it. A physician-led practice includes a health screening and a physician order. Some lounges do far less.
  • Who places and monitors the IV. Licensed clinicians with training in IV care, and monitoring during the drip, are part of what you pay for.
  • What is in the bag. The ingredients and amounts affect the cost.
  • Setting. Hospital, clinic, lounge or mobile service each carry different overhead.
  • Add-ons. Lab tests or a separate physician visit, if needed, are their own services.

What is usually not worth paying for

Be cautious about packages or memberships that commit you to many drips before anyone has checked whether you need them. Add-ons sold as "boosters" are another place costs climb without clear evidence of benefit. If you have symptoms such as ongoing tiredness, a physician visit and basic blood work may answer more of your questions than repeated drips. Some people are surprised to learn that a blood test can show whether they are low in B12 at all.

A lower price is not always a better deal. Ask any provider who screens you, who orders the IV, and who is on site if you have a reaction.

Safety

Is IV Therapy Safe?

For most screened adults, a vitamin IV given by trained clinicians is low risk. But "low risk" is not the same as no risk. Any time a needle enters a vein, there is a chance of bruising, irritation or infection. Any time fluid and nutrients go straight into the bloodstream, the body has to handle them quickly, which can be a problem for people with heart or kidney conditions.

Regulators have also raised concerns about the IV drip industry. The Federal Trade Commission has taken action against IV businesses for health claims they could not support, and it requires competent and reliable scientific evidence for health claims. California expects an appropriate assessment by a licensed prescriber before IV treatment. That is why we screen every patient and why we do not advertise IVs as a fix for illness, tiredness or hangovers.

What makes an IV safer

  • A health history and medication review before every first drip, and an update at later visits
  • Sterile technique and single-use supplies
  • Ingredients from licensed pharmacies, with labels you can read
  • Infusing at an appropriate rate, with a clinician watching for reactions
  • A clear plan for what happens if you feel unwell, including calling 911 when needed

After your IV

Keep the bandage on for a short while and watch the site over the next day or two. Call us if you notice spreading redness, warmth, swelling, a hard painful cord along the vein, or fever. Get emergency care for hives, swelling of the lips or face, or trouble breathing.

Keep the benefit side honest, too: evidence that IV vitamins help people who are not deficient is limited. When the likely benefit is small, even small risks deserve attention.

Not for Everyone

Who Should Not Have IV Therapy

We generally will not give an elective vitamin IV, or we will check with your own doctor first, if you have:

  • Heart failure or a heart condition that limits how much fluid you can handle
  • Kidney disease or reduced kidney function
  • Serious liver disease
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding, unless your obstetric clinician agrees
  • Uncontrolled high blood pressure
  • An allergy to any ingredient, or a past reaction to IV vitamins
  • Leber's disease (an inherited eye condition), because of the B12 form used in some blends
  • Active cancer treatment, unless your oncologist approves

Also skip a drip and get medical care instead if you are acutely ill with fever, chest pain, trouble breathing, confusion, severe vomiting or signs of serious dehydration. Those need an emergency room or urgent care, not a wellness IV.

Questions to Ask

How to Choose an IV Therapy Provider

  • Is there a health screening before the IV, reviewed by a licensed prescriber?
  • Who places the IV, and who is on site if you react?
  • Can they tell you exactly what is in the bag, and the amounts?
  • Are their claims modest? Be cautious of promises about immunity, hangovers, weight loss or skin.
  • Will they say no when an IV is not right for you?

Questions

IV Therapy Cost and Safety: FAQs

How much is an IV drip?

Prices vary by provider, setting and ingredients. Call us to ask about current pricing and payment options; we review costs with you before treatment.

What are the most common side effects?

Bruising or soreness at the IV site, a cool feeling in the arm, flushing and lightheadedness. Vein irritation, infection and allergic reactions are less common.

Can IV therapy be dangerous?

Rarely, yes, especially for people with heart or kidney disease, or when screening and sterile technique are skipped. That is why a physician screening comes first.

Can I get IV therapy while pregnant?

Not as an elective wellness drip without your obstetric clinician's agreement. Safety of many IV ingredients in pregnancy is not well studied.

Is IV better than drinking water and taking vitamins?

For most healthy people who can eat and drink normally, there is no good evidence that it is. IVs have a clear role when someone cannot drink enough or has a documented deficiency.
