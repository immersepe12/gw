The Basics

What Is Occupational Therapy?

In occupational therapy, "occupation" doesn't just mean a job. It means anything you need or want to do in a day. For a child, that's playing, holding a pencil, getting dressed, eating with a spoon and coping in a busy classroom. For an adult, it might be buttoning a shirt after a hand injury, cooking safely, typing at work or getting back to a hobby.

A licensed occupational therapist (OT) looks at what is getting in the way: weakness, limited motion, poor coordination, pain, fatigue, sensory difficulties or the setup of your home or workspace. Then they help in three main ways:

  • Building skills, such as hand strength, fine motor control or planning the steps of a task.
  • Changing the task, breaking it into smaller steps or teaching a different way to do it.
  • Changing the environment, with tools, adaptive equipment ideas or changes at home, school or work.

The American Occupational Therapy Association describes OT as helping people participate in the things they want and need to do through the therapeutic use of everyday activities. That focus on daily life is what sets OT apart.

Pediatric OT

Occupational Therapy for Kids

Pediatric occupational therapy helps children build the skills they need for play, learning and self-care. Every child develops at their own pace, so these are reasons to ask, not signs that something is wrong. Parents often reach out when a child:

  • Struggles to hold a crayon or pencil, use scissors or manage buttons and zippers well behind peers.
  • Has handwriting that is very hard to read or tiring to produce.
  • Is extremely bothered by noise, clothing tags, haircuts or certain food textures, or seems to crave constant movement.
  • Has trouble with dressing, feeding or toileting routines for their age.
  • Has a hard time calming down, shifting between activities or staying organized.

What pediatric OT sessions look like

Sessions are built around play. A child might build with putty, thread beads, play catch, climb, or practice dressing a doll, each chosen to work on a specific skill. Parents learn strategies to use at home, and the therapist can share ideas that fit the classroom with your permission.

OT does not diagnose conditions like ADHD or autism, and it doesn't make those conditions go away. It helps children build skills and find strategies that make daily life easier. Learn more in our guide to sensory processing and fine motor help.

Free routes for children

Children under 3 may qualify for free evaluations and services through California Early Start, reached in Stanislaus County through Valley Mountain Regional Center. Children 3 and older can be evaluated at no cost by their public school district under the federal IDEA law. School-based OT focuses on what a child needs to take part in school. Clinic OT can work alongside it.

Adult OT

Occupational Therapy for Adults

For adults, outpatient OT often focuses on the hands and arms and on everyday independence. Common reasons adults come to OT include:

Hand, wrist and elbow problems

Carpal tunnel symptoms, tendon problems, stiffness after a fracture, and recovery after hand surgery, when your surgeon orders therapy. Read our guide to hand therapy and carpal tunnel.

Daily living after illness or injury

Relearning or adapting tasks like dressing, bathing and cooking after a hospital stay, surgery or a change in health, with ideas to make home routines safer.

Living with a chronic condition

Arthritis, chronic pain or fatigue can make everyday tasks harder. OT can teach joint-protection and energy-saving strategies so you can keep doing what matters to you. These often fit well with our pain management and physical therapy services.

Getting back to work

Practicing the hand and arm tasks your job requires, and looking at how your workstation or tools are set up.

See a doctor first, before starting therapy, if you have numbness that is constant or getting worse, sudden weakness, a hand that is cold, pale or very swollen, signs of infection after surgery, or pain after a fall or injury that might be a fracture.

Occupational Therapy vs. Physical Therapy

How occupational therapy and physical therapy usually differ. Many people benefit from both.
Occupational therapyPhysical therapy
Main focusEveryday tasks and independenceMovement, strength, balance and mobility
Typical body areasHands, arms, fine motor control, sensory needsBack, neck, hips, knees, shoulders, walking
Example goalsWrite a sentence, button a coat, cook a meal safelyWalk without a limp, climb stairs, return to sport
For childrenFine motor, self-care, sensory and play skillsRolling, sitting, walking, running and balance

Because both are offered in one physician-led practice, your therapists can share a single care plan and talk to each other directly. For children's movement milestones, see pediatric physical therapy. For communication, see pediatric speech therapy.

Do you need a referral for OT?

California has a specific direct-access law for physical therapy, but not an equivalent one that spells this out for occupational therapy. In practice, a physician referral or order may be required by your health plan or for certain kinds of care. Having a physician in the same practice can make that step simpler. Questions

Occupational Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

What does an occupational therapist do for a child?

They use play-based activities to build fine motor, self-care, sensory and organizing skills, and teach parents strategies to use at home.

Do I need a referral for occupational therapy in California?

A physician referral or order may be required by your plan or for certain care. Call us and we'll explain what applies to your situation.

What is the difference between OT and PT?

OT focuses on everyday tasks and hand skills. PT focuses on movement, strength, balance and walking. Many people benefit from both.

Does occupational therapy help with handwriting?

Handwriting is a common OT goal. Therapists work on grip, hand strength, posture, letter formation and visual-motor skills.

Can adults get occupational therapy?

Yes. Adults come to OT for hand and wrist problems, recovery after surgery or illness, and help with daily tasks when living with a chronic condition.
