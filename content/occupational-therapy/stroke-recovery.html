Act Fast

Know the Signs of a Stroke: BE FAST

Before anything else: a new stroke is an emergency. Treatment works best in the first hours, so never wait to see if symptoms pass. Use the BE FAST checklist:

  • Balance: sudden loss of balance or dizziness.
  • Eyes: sudden trouble seeing in one or both eyes.
  • Face: one side of the face droops when the person smiles.
  • Arm: one arm drifts down or feels weak or numb.
  • Speech: slurred, strange or hard-to-understand speech.
  • Time: call 911 right away and note when symptoms started.

If someone recovering from a stroke shows any of these signs again, call 911. Do not drive them to our clinic. Our practice provides outpatient therapy only, once the hospital team says it is time for rehab at home and in the community.

The Basics

What Occupational Therapy Does After a Stroke

A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is blocked or a blood vessel bleeds. Depending on the area affected, it can change strength, feeling, coordination, vision, memory, attention and energy. Physical therapy usually focuses on walking and balance. Occupational therapy focuses on the things you do all day: getting dressed, bathing, making a meal, managing medicines, writing, using a phone and getting back to hobbies or work.

The American Heart Association and American Stroke Association guideline for adult stroke rehabilitation (2016) recommends that people who have had a stroke get rehab matched to their needs, including practice of real daily activities. It supports task-specific training, meaning you practice the actual skill you want back, many times, with the task gradually made harder as you improve.

That is the heart of OT. Rather than generic exercises alone, your occupational therapist builds practice around your goals, whether that is buttoning a shirt, cutting vegetables or holding a grandchild safely.

Upper Limb

Arm Recovery After Stroke

Weakness on one side of the body is one of the most common effects of stroke, and the arm and hand often recover more slowly than the leg. Recovery is different for every person. Many people see the biggest changes in the first few months, and some keep improving with practice long after that. Nobody can promise exactly how much movement will return or how fast.

Approaches your therapist may use

  • Repetitive task practice: reaching, grasping and releasing everyday objects many times, in ways that feel meaningful.
  • Strengthening and range of motion: keeping the shoulder, wrist and fingers moving and building strength where it is returning.
  • Constraint-induced movement ideas: for some people with enough movement, encouraging the weaker arm by limiting use of the stronger one during practice. The AHA/ASA guideline lists this as a reasonable option for selected patients.
  • Mirror therapy and mental practice: low-cost techniques the guideline describes as reasonable additions to regular therapy.
  • Shoulder care and positioning: protecting a weak shoulder to lower the chance of pain.
  • Adaptive tools: built-up utensils, button hooks or one-handed techniques so you can stay independent while recovery continues.

If fine finger control or hand stiffness is a main concern, our page on hand therapy explains more about that kind of work.

Daily Life

Home Safety, Energy and Thinking Skills

Falls are common after a stroke. Your OT can walk through your home routines with you and suggest practical changes, such as grab bars by the toilet and tub, a shower chair, better lighting, removing loose rugs and placing everyday items within easy reach. For walking and balance, OT works alongside balance and fall prevention physical therapy.

Managing post-stroke fatigue

Many people are surprised by how tired they feel after a stroke, even months later. OT teaches energy-saving strategies: planning the hardest tasks for your best time of day, sitting for grooming or cooking, breaking jobs into smaller steps and building rest into the day without giving up activity.

Attention, memory and vision

A stroke can also affect concentration, memory, planning or noticing things on one side. Your therapist may use checklists, phone reminders, pill organizers and scanning practice so routines feel more manageable. Questions about driving go to your physician, who can advise on whether a formal driving evaluation is needed.

Family

Caregiver Training and Emotional Health

Stroke recovery happens at home far more than in a clinic. Family members are welcome at visits, and your OT can show them how to help safely with transfers, dressing and bathing, how much to help and when to step back, and how to keep practice going between sessions. Caregivers need care too, so ask us about pacing yourself.

Depression and anxiety are common after a stroke and can slow recovery if they go unnoticed. If you or your loved one feels low, worried or not like yourself, tell your physician. Our licensed counselors offer talk therapy through counseling at our practice, and medication questions go to a physician.

Questions

Occupational Therapy After Stroke: Frequently Asked Questions

When should OT start after a stroke?

Rehab usually begins in the hospital once you are medically stable. Outpatient OT, like ours, typically follows after discharge from the hospital or an inpatient rehab unit. Your medical team will tell you when it is safe. If you finished therapy earlier and still have goals, you can ask your physician whether more outpatient therapy makes sense now.

Can my arm still improve months after a stroke?

Many people make the fastest gains early, but some keep improving with focused practice well beyond the first months. How much returns varies a great deal from person to person, and no one can promise a result. Your occupational therapist will test your current movement and set realistic goals, including tools that help you stay independent meanwhile.

How is OT different from physical therapy after a stroke?

Physical therapy usually focuses on walking, balance, leg strength and moving safely. Occupational therapy focuses on daily activities like dressing, bathing, cooking and writing, plus arm and hand use, thinking skills and home setup. Many stroke survivors benefit from both, and in our practice the two teams share one care plan so goals line up.

Can family members come to therapy sessions?

Yes, and we encourage it. Caregivers learn safe ways to help with transfers, dressing and bathing, and how to support practice at home without doing everything for their loved one. Bringing a family member also helps when there is a lot of information to remember. Let us know ahead of time who will be joining you.

What helps with tiredness after a stroke?

Post-stroke fatigue is very common. OT can help you plan your day, pace demanding tasks, sit for jobs like grooming, and balance rest with activity. Tell your physician about fatigue too, since sleep problems, mood changes, medicines or other health issues can play a part and may need their own evaluation.
