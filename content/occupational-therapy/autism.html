The Basics

Autism and Occupational Therapy: Our Approach

Autism spectrum disorder is a developmental difference in how a person communicates, interacts and experiences the world. The CDC estimates that about 1 in 31 eight-year-olds in the communities it tracks were identified as autistic in 2022. Every autistic child is different, with their own strengths, interests and challenges.

Autism is lifelong, and therapy does not make it go away. We do not promise to "fix" autism, and we do not try to stop harmless behaviors like stimming or flapping that help a child feel regulated. Instead, occupational therapy focuses on goals that matter to your child and family, such as getting dressed, joining family meals, coping with a busy store or using a pencil at school.

Autism is diagnosed by a qualified professional, such as a developmental pediatrician, psychologist or specialized team. If you have concerns but no diagnosis, start with your pediatrician. An OT evaluation is not an autism diagnosis.

Independence

Building Daily Living Skills

"Occupations" in OT are the everyday activities of life. For children, that means self-care, play, school and rest. Autistic children may find some of these hard because of motor planning, sensory discomfort, changes in routine or the number of steps involved.

An occupational therapist may help with:

  • Dressing: putting on clothes in order, managing fasteners and finding comfortable clothing.
  • Toileting routines: breaking steps into a visual sequence and practicing them calmly.
  • Grooming: tooth brushing, hair washing, haircuts and nail trims, often by slowly building tolerance and predictability.
  • Sleep routines: a calm, consistent bedtime sequence.
  • Transitions: visual schedules, timers and first-then boards to make changes more predictable.

Picky eating and feeding concerns can be complex. If your child eats a very limited diet, is losing weight or gags or chokes often, talk to your pediatrician first, who may refer you to a feeding specialist.

Honest Evidence

Sensory Supports for Autistic Kids

Sensory differences are part of how autism is described in the DSM-5. A child may be bothered by sounds, lights, textures or smells, or may seek out movement, pressure or certain sensations.

Here is the honest picture on sensory-based therapy:

  • Ayres Sensory Integration, a structured, play-based approach delivered by trained OTs, has some support from small randomized trials in autistic children, focused on progress toward individual daily-living goals.
  • Single sensory tools, such as weighted vests or brushing on their own, have weaker or inconsistent evidence.

Practical supports often help, whatever the research debate: noise-reducing headphones, a quiet corner, soft clothing, warning before loud events and planned movement breaks. We focus on what helps your child take part in daily life, track whether it is working, and adjust. For more background, see our page on sensory processing in children.

Hands and Body

Fine Motor, Motor Planning and Play

Many autistic children also have differences in coordination and motor planning, which is figuring out how to sequence a new movement. This can affect handwriting, using scissors, catching a ball, riding a bike or learning a new playground game.

OT builds these skills through your child's interests whenever possible. A child who loves trains might practice fine motor skills by connecting track pieces or drawing a train schedule. Building on interests tends to keep children engaged and makes practice feel like play. For typical age ranges, see fine motor skills by age.

Communication and OT goals often go hand in hand. If your child is working on communication, including speech, signs, pictures or a device, see communication support for autistic children. Our speech and OT teams can share one plan.

Try at Home

Autism OT Activities to Try at Home

Simple ideas many families find useful. Follow your child's lead, and stop if something causes distress.

  • A picture schedule for the morning or bedtime routine.
  • A calm-down space with soft cushions, a favorite book and headphones.
  • Heavy-work play like pushing a laundry basket, carrying groceries or wall push-ups before a hard task.
  • Play dough, sticker books and building toys tied to your child's interests.
  • Practicing new experiences, like a haircut, in small steps with lots of warning.
  • Choosing clothes without tags or seams if textures bother your child.

Questions

Occupational Therapy for Autism: Frequently Asked Questions

Can occupational therapy help an autistic child?

Many autistic children benefit from OT for daily living skills, sensory comfort, fine motor skills and routines. OT does not change autism itself, and that is not the goal. Instead, it aims to make everyday activities easier and more comfortable. Your child's therapist will set specific goals with you and check progress together regularly.

Does my child need an autism diagnosis to start OT?

No. OT is based on the daily skills your child is finding hard, not on a label. If you suspect autism, talk to your pediatrician about a diagnostic evaluation, because a diagnosis can open doors to other supports. OT and the diagnostic process can happen at the same time, so you do not have to wait.

Will OT try to stop my child from stimming?

Not if it is harmless. Stimming, like rocking or hand flapping, often helps autistic people feel calm or express joy. We focus on behaviors that cause injury or truly get in the way of daily life, and even then we look for safer ways to meet the same need rather than simply stopping it.

Is sensory integration therapy proven for autism?

Evidence is mixed. Structured Ayres Sensory Integration has support from small randomized trials in autistic children for progress on individual goals. Single tools like weighted vests have weaker evidence. We tie any sensory strategy to a clear daily-life goal and review with you whether it is actually helping your child.

How do OT and speech therapy work together for autism?

Speech therapy supports communication, whether through speech, signs, pictures or a device. OT supports daily living, sensory comfort and motor skills. They often overlap, for example using a picture schedule in both. In our practice, both teams can share one care plan so your child gets consistent strategies across visits and at home.
