Background

Why Athletes Use Hyperbaric Chambers

There have been widely publicized reports of professional athletes using hyperbaric chambers as part of their routines. Those reports don't tell us whether the chambers made any difference, and they aren't evidence.

The reasons usually given are appealing: more oxygen delivered to tired muscles, less inflammation, and faster clearing of the by-products of hard exercise. These ideas are plausible enough that researchers have tested them, and those tests are summarized below.

The pressure mismatch

Here is the detail that matters most. The chambers athletes use at home or in training facilities are usually soft-sided units that reach about 1.3 ATA (atmospheres absolute), often with oxygen from a concentrator. Medical HBOT is delivered in hard-shell chambers, and almost all of the recovery trials used medical pressures of 2.0 to 2.4 ATA.

That means a positive result from a 2.4 ATA trial tells you very little about a 1.3 ATA soft chamber, and vice versa. Read more about soft chambers at 1.3 ATA vs. hard-shell medical chambers and the trade-offs of home chambers.

Making sense of the mixed picture

The older Cochrane review and the newer meta-analysis don't quite contradict each other. They mostly measured different things. The Cochrane review looked at pain and real injuries like sprains, and found nothing helpful. The 2025 analysis looked mainly at blood markers of muscle damage and soreness scores after hard exercise, and reported faster return to baseline. A faster drop in a lab marker is interesting, but it is not the same as healing an injury faster or playing better. For a quick guide to judging any study, see how to read an HBOT study.

The Limits

What the Research Does Not Show

  • Not FDA clearance. Sports injuries are named by the FDA as not cleared.
  • Not that soft 1.3 ATA chambers do anything measurable. Almost all trials used medical chambers at 2.0 ATA or higher.
  • Not better athletic performance. The 2021 meta-analysis found no consistent performance effect.
  • Not faster ligament or tendon healing. Evidence is inconclusive at best, and Cochrane found no benefit for sprains.
  • Not that lower lactate means more games won. Lab markers don't automatically translate into results on the field.
  • Not safety of frequent, long-term use in healthy people. Repeated exposure carries risks, including ear and sinus barotrauma, temporary nearsightedness and, rarely, oxygen toxicity seizures. Read about side effects that accumulate with repeated exposure.
  • Not a comparison with other recovery methods. There is no head-to-head evidence comparing HBOT with cryotherapy, compression or sleep, so no claim of equivalence or superiority can be made.

The Cleared Side

When an Athlete Does Have a Cleared Indication

Athletes are people first, and injuries sometimes lead to conditions that are on the cleared list. When that happens, HBOT may support care as part of a physician-directed plan. Examples include:

  • A severe crush injury, managed first in hospital. Read about crush injuries and acute traumatic ischemia.
  • Chronic bone infection that hasn't responded to standard care after an open fracture. See chronic refractory osteomyelitis.
  • A skin graft or flap in trouble after a skin-loss injury. See compromised skin grafts and flaps.
  • A surgical wound that won't heal in someone with a complicating factor. See non-healing wounds.

These are assessed on referral, on their own merits, and HBOT is offered only when a physician determines it is appropriate.

Questions

HBOT for Athletes: Frequently Asked Questions

Why do athletes use hyperbaric chambers?

The reasons usually given are faster recovery and less soreness. Most use soft chambers at low pressure, and controlled evidence for benefit is limited and mixed.

Does HBOT help muscle recovery after exercise?

A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 small studies reported faster recovery of muscle-damage markers. A Cochrane review found no benefit for soreness, and possibly more pain. It is not a cleared indication.

Does hyperbaric oxygen help sprains or ACL recovery?

Cochrane found no benefit for ankle or knee ligament sprains. There are no controlled trials for recovery after ACL reconstruction.

Is HBOT for sports injuries FDA-cleared?

No. The FDA lists sports injuries among uses hyperbaric chambers are not cleared for.

Do athletes' home chambers work like medical HBOT?

No. Home soft chambers run at about 1.3 ATA, well below the 2.0 to 2.4 ATA used in medical treatment and in nearly all recovery trials.
