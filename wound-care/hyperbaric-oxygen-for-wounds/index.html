Wound Care

Hyperbaric Oxygen for Wound Healing: When It's Used

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is used as an add-on to standard wound care for a short list of FDA-cleared indications, such as selected diabetic foot ulcers and radiation tissue injury. It is not used for venous leg ulcers or pressure injuries. Your physician decides whether it is appropriate for your wound.

The Basics

How Hyperbaric Oxygen Relates to Wound Healing

In hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT), you breathe close to 100% oxygen inside a chamber pressurized above normal air pressure. Under pressure, much more oxygen dissolves into the blood plasma and can reach tissue where blood flow is poor. Healing tissue needs oxygen to build new blood vessels, make collagen and help white blood cells fight bacteria. In some wounds, low oxygen in the tissue is part of why healing stalls.

That is the reasoning. The practical question is narrower: for which wounds is there enough evidence to use it? The FDA and the Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS) recognize a specific list. At our practice, HBOT is given in hard-shell monoplace chambers for FDA-cleared indications only. Learn more about hyperbaric oxygen therapy at our practice.

Acute emergencies such as crush injuries, necrotizing infections and severe burns are also on the indication list, but they are treated in hospitals, not at our outpatient practice.

Not Indications

Wounds HBOT Is Not Used For

  • Venous leg ulcers. Not an HBOT indication. The problem is vein pressure and swelling, and compression is the main treatment. See venous leg ulcers.
  • Pressure injuries (bed sores). Not an HBOT indication. Pressure relief and wound care are the treatments. See pressure injuries.
  • Most surgical wounds, unless they involve a compromised graft or flap or irradiated tissue. See surgical wounds.
  • Simple cuts, scrapes and wounds that are healing on schedule.
  • Wounds with untreated major artery blockage, where restoring blood flow, usually by a vascular specialist, comes first.

Medicare's national coverage policy for HBOT also lists only specific conditions. Being honest about where HBOT does not fit is part of using it responsibly.

Our Approach

How HBOT Fits With Standard Wound Care

HBOT is an adjunct. It is added to good wound care; it does not replace it. For a diabetic foot ulcer, for example, offloading pressure, removing dead tissue, controlling infection and blood sugar, and checking blood flow all continue during HBOT.

  1. Wound assessment by a licensed clinician at our wound care service.
  2. Physician review of whether your wound meets an FDA-cleared indication and whether HBOT is safe for you.
  3. If appropriate, a course of sessions, commonly 20 to 40, usually on weekdays, with progress reviewed along the way.
  4. If not appropriate, we explain why and keep working on the wound with standard care.

Being able to offer wound care and HBOT in the same Modesto practice means one shared plan and fewer handoffs. We see patients from Modesto, Turlock, Manteca, Ripon and across the Central Valley.

The Evidence

What the Research Shows

The strongest research on HBOT for chronic wounds is in diabetic foot ulcers. A Cochrane review of randomized trials (Kranke and colleagues, 2015) found that HBOT may improve the chance of healing diabetic foot ulcers in the short term, at around six weeks. The same review found the longer-term benefit less certain, noted that many trials were small and had limitations, and found too little evidence to draw conclusions for venous, arterial or pressure ulcers.

In plain terms: for the right diabetic foot ulcer, alongside good standard care, HBOT may help. It is not a shortcut, it does not work for every patient, and it should not be used for wounds outside the indication list. Your physician will talk through what the evidence means for your specific wound.

What helps decide if HBOT is likely to be useful

Before recommending HBOT, physicians often look at whether the wound has stalled despite at least a month of good standard care, whether major blood flow problems have been addressed, and sometimes measure oxygen levels in the skin near the wound. These checks help pick the patients most likely to benefit and avoid sessions that are unlikely to help.

Safety

Side Effects and Who Should Not Have HBOT

The most common side effect is ear pressure or pain, similar to flying. Others include sinus pressure, temporary changes in near vision, tiredness and, rarely, low blood sugar in people with diabetes, lung problems or seizures from oxygen. An untreated collapsed lung is a reason not to have HBOT, and some medicines and conditions need extra care. A safety screening happens before your first session.

A session usually takes about 90 minutes to two hours, including pressurizing and depressurizing. You lie in the chamber, can talk with staff, and can rest. If you have diabetes, blood sugar is typically checked around each session because HBOT can lower it.

Questions

HBOT for Wounds: Frequently Asked Questions

Does hyperbaric oxygen help wounds heal?

For certain cleared indications, such as selected diabetic foot ulcers, studies suggest it may support healing as an add-on to good care. It is not used for most wounds.

Is HBOT used for venous or pressure ulcers?

No. Neither is an HBOT indication. Compression and pressure relief are the main treatments.

How many sessions are needed for a wound?

A course is commonly 20 to 40 sessions, set and adjusted by your physician based on how the wound responds.

Can I get HBOT without wound care?

No. HBOT works as an adjunct. Standard wound care continues throughout, whether with us or with your current wound team.
