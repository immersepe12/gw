Wound Care

Pressure Injuries and Bed Sores: A Caregiver Guide

Pressure injuries, also called bed sores or pressure ulcers, form when steady pressure cuts off blood flow to skin over a bony area. Treatment centers on relieving pressure, caring for the wound, and good nutrition. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is not an indication for pressure injuries.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Rajwinder Singh Bahia, MD · October 24, 2026

Get same-day medical care if a pressure sore has spreading redness, a foul smell, pus, black tissue, or if the person has a fever, chills, confusion or seems much weaker. Deep sores can lead to serious infection. Call 911 if the person is very unwell. Our practice is outpatient and is not an emergency service.

The Basics

How Pressure Injuries Form

When someone lies or sits in one position for a long time, their body weight presses skin against bone. That pressure squeezes the small blood vessels shut. Without blood flow, skin and the tissue beneath it can be damaged in a matter of hours. Sliding down in a bed or chair adds friction and shear, which tear at deeper tissue.

The most common spots are the tailbone and buttocks, hips, heels, ankles, shoulder blades and the back of the head.

Who is at risk

  • People who are bedbound or use a wheelchair
  • People recovering from surgery, stroke or serious illness
  • People with reduced feeling, such as from spinal cord injury or diabetic nerve damage
  • People with incontinence, which keeps skin moist and fragile
  • Older adults with thin skin and poor nutrition

Stages

The Stages of Pressure Injuries

Pressure injury stages, as described by the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel
StageWhat you may see
Stage 1Intact skin with a red area that does not turn white when pressed. On darker skin it may look purple, blue or just different, and feel warm, firm or soft.
Stage 2A shallow open sore or a blister. The wound bed is pink or red.
Stage 3A deeper wound through the skin into fat. It may look like a crater.
Stage 4A deep wound exposing muscle, tendon or bone.
UnstageableThe base is covered by yellow, tan, brown or black tissue, so depth can't be seen.
Deep tissue injuryA dark purple or maroon area, or a blood-filled blister, over intact skin.

Stage 1 and deep tissue injuries can get worse quickly, so call early. Stage 3 and 4 sores, and any sore with signs of infection, need prompt medical care.

For Caregivers

Preventing Pressure Injuries at Home

  • Change position often. A common guide is about every two hours in bed and more often in a chair, but ask your clinician what fits the person you care for.
  • Float the heels with a pillow under the calves so heels don't touch the mattress.
  • Use pressure-relieving surfaces, such as special mattresses or cushions, when recommended.
  • Lift, don't drag, when moving someone, to avoid friction and shear.
  • Keep skin clean and dry, and change soiled pads promptly.
  • Check the skin daily, especially over bony areas.
  • Support nutrition and fluids, with enough protein.

An occupational therapist can help with positioning, seating and safe transfers at home.

Treatment

How Pressure Injuries Are Treated

  1. Take pressure off the wound. This is the single most important step. Without it, a sore won't heal.
  2. Wound care. Cleaning, the right dressings, and removing dead tissue when needed.
  3. Treat infection if present. Deep sores can involve bone (osteomyelitis), which needs specific evaluation.
  4. Nutrition and overall health, including protein, fluids and blood sugar control.
  5. Surgery for some deep stage 3 or 4 wounds, through a surgical referral.

Caring for the wound between visits

Your clinician will show you how to clean the sore and change the dressing, and how often. Keep the area free of pressure as much as possible, including when the person is sitting up in bed, which puts weight on the tailbone. Keep a simple log of position changes, dressing changes, eating and drinking. It helps you spot patterns and gives the care team useful information at each visit.

Food and fluids matter

Healing a pressure injury uses a lot of protein and calories. If the person you care for is eating poorly, losing weight or not drinking much, tell the care team. Small, frequent meals, protein-rich snacks and help at mealtimes can make a difference, and the physician can check for problems that affect appetite.

Caring for yourself as a caregiver

Turning someone every few hours, day and night, is hard work. If you are caring for a family member at home, ask about help with lifting and transfers, equipment that reduces strain on your back, and local caregiver support. A plan that you can keep up is better than a perfect plan that wears you out.

At our physician-led practice in downtown Modesto, a licensed clinician assesses the wound and works with you and the person's other doctors on a plan. Caregivers are welcome at every visit.

Hyperbaric Oxygen

Is Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Used for Bed Sores?

No. Pressure injuries are not an HBOT indication. They are not on the Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society's accepted list, and Medicare's national coverage policy does not include them. Pressure relief and wound care are what work. One narrow exception to know about: if a deep sore has led to chronic bone infection that hasn't responded to standard treatment, refractory osteomyelitis is a separate, cleared indication, and your physician would evaluate that on its own terms.

Questions

Pressure Injuries: Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best treatment for a bed sore?

Taking pressure off the area, combined with proper wound care and good nutrition. Deep or infected sores need medical care.

How fast can a bed sore develop?

Skin damage can begin within hours of unrelieved pressure, which is why regular position changes matter.

How do I spot a pressure injury on darker skin?

Redness may not show. Look for purple, blue or darker patches, and feel for areas that are warmer, cooler, firmer or softer than nearby skin.

Does hyperbaric oxygen help pressure ulcers?

Pressure ulcers are not an HBOT indication. Pressure relief and wound care are the treatments.
