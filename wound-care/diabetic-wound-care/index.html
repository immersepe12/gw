Wound Care

Diabetic Wound Care and Foot Care

Diabetic wound care starts with daily foot checks, good footwear and keeping pressure off any sore. Blood sugar control, infection treatment and healthy blood flow all affect healing. Spreading redness, fever, pus or dark skin on your foot needs same-day medical care.

Safety First

Diabetic Foot Warning Signs That Need Care Today

With diabetes, small foot problems can turn serious fast. Get same-day medical care if you notice:

  • Redness, warmth or swelling that is spreading
  • Fever or chills with a foot wound
  • Pus, drainage or a foul odor
  • Skin turning black, blue or gray
  • A new wound you cannot see the bottom of, or one that reaches bone
  • A suddenly hot, swollen, red foot, even without a wound, which can signal Charcot foot

Go to the emergency room if you have these signs with high fever, confusion or high blood sugar you cannot bring down.

The Background

Why Diabetic Wounds Heal Slowly

Diabetes affects your feet in three main ways. Nerve damage (neuropathy) can numb your feet, so you may not feel a blister, cut or stone in your shoe. Poor circulation reduces the blood that carries oxygen and healing cells to a wound. And high blood sugar weakens the immune response and slows tissue repair.

Together, these make foot ulcers a common diabetes complication. The CDC and NIDDK note that good daily foot care and blood sugar management can prevent many of the problems that lead to ulcers and amputation.

Every Day

Daily Diabetic Foot Care Checks

A daily check takes two minutes and is one of the most useful habits you can build:

  • Look at the tops, soles, heels and between the toes every day. Use a mirror or ask a family member if you cannot see well.
  • Look for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, calluses, nail problems or color changes.
  • Wash your feet daily in lukewarm water, testing the temperature with your elbow, and dry well between the toes.
  • Moisturize the tops and bottoms, but not between the toes.
  • Trim nails straight across, or have a foot care professional do it.
  • Do not cut corns or calluses yourself or use over-the-counter wart or corn removers.

Taking the Pressure Off

Footwear and Offloading for Diabetic Wounds

Everyday footwear

Wear shoes and socks at all times, even indoors. Choose shoes that fit well with room for your toes, and check inside each shoe for stones or rough seams before putting it on. Avoid going barefoot on hot pavement or sand, a real risk during Central Valley summers. People at higher risk may be prescribed therapeutic shoes or custom inserts.

Offloading an ulcer

If you already have an ulcer, the most important step is taking pressure off it. This is called offloading. International guidelines from the IWGDF recommend devices such as a total contact cast or a removable boot made to stay on, depending on the wound. Walking on an ulcer, even briefly, can undo days of healing. Your care team will tell you how much you can walk and what to wear.

Inside Out

Blood Sugar and Wound Healing

Keeping blood sugar in your target range helps your body fight infection and repair tissue. Work with your primary care physician or endocrinologist on your A1C goal, medicines and diet. Stopping smoking improves circulation to your feet, and eating enough protein supports healing. If you notice your sugars climbing while you have a wound, tell your care team, as infection can raise blood sugar.

Team Care

The Diabetic Foot Care Team

Diabetic foot wounds often need several kinds of expertise working together:

  • Podiatry for wound cleaning, removing dead tissue (debridement), offloading and footwear
  • Vascular specialists to check blood flow and, if needed, restore it
  • Your primary care physician or endocrinologist for blood sugar and overall health
  • Infectious disease specialists when infection reaches deep tissue or bone
  • Wound care clinicians for dressings and regular wound checks

A physician-led practice can help coordinate these pieces so nothing falls through the cracks.

When hyperbaric oxygen is considered

For some people, a serious diabetic foot ulcer that has not healed despite good standard care may qualify for hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) as an add-on, never a replacement. It is an FDA-cleared indication for certain ulcers, typically deeper wounds (often Wagner grade 3 or higher). Read the details on our diabetic foot ulcer page.

Questions

Diabetic Wound Care: Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I check my feet if I have diabetes?

Every day. Look at the tops, soles, heels and between your toes for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling or color changes. Use a mirror or ask someone to help if you cannot see well. The ADA also recommends a full foot exam by a clinician at least once a year, and more often if you have neuropathy or a past ulcer.

Why won't my diabetic foot wound heal?

Common reasons include ongoing pressure on the wound, poor blood flow, infection, and high blood sugar. Nerve damage can also mean you keep walking on the wound without feeling it. A wound that is not clearly smaller after about four weeks of good care should be re-evaluated, often including a check of circulation and testing for infection.

What shoes are best for diabetic feet?

Choose shoes that fit well, with a wide toe box, firm support and soft, seamless linings. Wear socks without tight bands, and check inside your shoes before putting them on. Avoid sandals that rub and going barefoot. If you have neuropathy, deformities or a past ulcer, ask about therapeutic shoes or custom inserts.

Can hyperbaric oxygen help a diabetic foot ulcer?

For some deeper ulcers that have not healed with good standard care, HBOT is an FDA-cleared add-on therapy. It does not replace offloading, infection control, blood flow treatment or blood sugar management. A physician decides whether you qualify. Our diabetic foot ulcer page explains who may be a candidate and what a course of treatment involves.

When is a diabetic foot problem an emergency?

Seek care the same day for spreading redness, warmth or swelling, pus or a foul smell, black or gray skin, or a wound that reaches deep tissue. Go to the emergency room if these come with fever, chills, confusion or blood sugar you cannot control. Fast treatment of foot infections helps lower the risk of amputation.
