Wound Care

Minor Burn Treatment and Wound Care

Most small burns from a stove, curling iron or hot pan heal at home with good first aid and clean dressings. This guide explains how to cool a burn, tell first degree from second degree burns, spot burns that need an ER or burn center, and when follow-up wound care helps.

Quick answer: Most small burns from a stove, curling iron or hot pan heal at home with good first aid. Cool the burn under cool running water for about 20 minutes, skip ice and butter, and cover it loosely. Large, deep, chemical or electrical burns need an ER. First Minutes

Burn First Aid: What to Do Right Away

  • Stop the burning. Move away from the heat source. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before it swells, unless clothing is stuck to the skin.
  • Cool it with running water. Hold the burn under cool, not icy, running tap water for about 20 minutes. Burn care groups recommend this as the most useful first step.
  • Skip ice, butter, oil and toothpaste. Ice can injure skin further, and home remedies trap heat and raise infection risk.
  • Cover loosely. Use a clean, non-stick dressing or plastic cling wrap laid over the burn, not wrapped tightly.
  • Ease the pain. Over-the-counter pain relievers such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen can help if they are safe for you. Check with a pharmacist if you are unsure.

Cooling helps even if you start a little late, so do not skip it. For large burns, cool the area but keep the person warm, because cooling a big area can drop body temperature, and call 911.

Burn Depth

First Degree vs. Second Degree Burns

Doctors describe burns by how deep they go. MedlinePlus and the National Institute of General Medical Sciences use these groups:

  • First degree (superficial): only the top layer of skin. It is red, dry and painful, like a mild sunburn, with no blisters. It usually heals in about a week without scarring.
  • Second degree (partial thickness): goes deeper. It blisters, looks wet or shiny, and hurts a lot. Small, shallow second degree burns often heal in two to three weeks. Deeper ones take longer and may scar.
  • Third degree (full thickness): destroys the full skin layer. It can look white, brown, leathery or charred, and may not hurt much because nerves are damaged. These always need emergency care.

Burns often change over the first day or two and can look deeper than they did at first. If you are not sure how deep a burn is, have it checked.

Know the Limits

When a Burn Needs the ER or a Burn Center

The American Burn Association lists burns that should be referred to a specialized burn center. Serious burns are handled at hospitals and burn centers, not at an outpatient wound clinic. Get emergency care for:

  • Second degree burns larger than about the size of your palm, or any third degree burn
  • Burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals, groin, or over a major joint such as the knee, elbow or shoulder
  • Burns that go all the way around an arm, leg, finger or the chest
  • Chemical burns, electrical burns (including from outlets or lightning) and inhalation injury from smoke or steam
  • Burns in infants and young children, older adults, or people with diabetes, heart disease or a weak immune system
  • Any burn you suspect was not an accident

For chemical burns, brush off dry chemicals and flush with lots of running water while help is on the way. For electrical burns, make sure the power is off before touching the person.

Some patients with severe burns receive hyperbaric oxygen as part of hospital burn care. Acute thermal burns are one of the FDA-cleared indications for hyperbaric oxygen therapy, decided by the burn team. Read more on our thermal burns and HBOT page; it is not a substitute for burn center care.

Day-to-Day Care

Caring for a Small Burn as It Heals

  • Wash gently once a day with mild soap and water, and pat dry.
  • Keep it covered and moist. A thin layer of petroleum jelly or an ointment your clinician recommends, under a non-stick dressing, helps the skin heal.
  • Leave blisters alone. Intact blisters protect the skin underneath. If one breaks on its own, clean it and keep it covered.
  • Protect new skin from the sun for months, with clothing or sunscreen once the skin has closed, to limit darkening.
  • Check your tetanus status. Ask your doctor if your last tetanus shot was more than five years ago.

Itching is common as burns heal. Moisturizer helps once the skin has closed. Avoid scratching, which can open the skin.

When follow-up wound care helps

A small burn that is not improving after a week or two, a blister that keeps reopening, or a burn on someone with diabetes or poor circulation deserves a closer look. At our Modesto wound clinic, a clinician can assess the burn, clean it, choose dressings, and coordinate with your doctor. If a burn needs a burn specialist, we refer you. We see patients from Modesto, Ceres and nearby towns.

Warning Signs

Signs a Burn May Be Infected

Get same-day medical care if you notice:

  • Redness that spreads beyond the burn or red streaks moving up the limb
  • Increasing pain, swelling or warmth after the first two days
  • Pus, cloudy drainage or a foul odor
  • Fever or chills
  • Skin that turns dark, black or dies off

Questions

Minor Burns: Frequently Asked Questions

Should I put ice on a burn?

No. Ice and ice water can damage already injured skin. Use cool running tap water for about 20 minutes instead. Also skip butter, oils, toothpaste and other home remedies, which hold in heat and can raise the risk of infection. After cooling, cover the burn loosely with a clean, non-stick dressing.

Should I pop a burn blister?

No. An intact blister acts like a natural bandage that protects healing skin from germs. If a blister breaks on its own, gently wash the area with mild soap and water, apply a thin layer of ointment, and cover it. Large or painful blisters should be looked at by a clinician.

How do I know if a burn is second degree?

Second degree burns usually blister, look wet or shiny, and are quite painful. First degree burns are red and dry with no blisters. Burns can look deeper after a day or two. Any second degree burn larger than your palm, or on the face, hands, feet, genitals or a joint, needs medical care.

How long does a minor burn take to heal?

A first degree burn often heals in about a week. A small, shallow second degree burn may take two to three weeks. Healing can be slower with diabetes, poor circulation, smoking or infection. If a burn is not clearly improving after one to two weeks, have a clinician take a look.

Can I come to your clinic instead of the ER for a burn?

Not for a new serious burn. Large, deep, chemical or electrical burns, and burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals or joints, need an emergency room or burn center. Our outpatient wound clinic can help with follow-up care for small burns that are slow to heal, after urgent problems have been ruled out.
