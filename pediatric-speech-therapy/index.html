The Basics

What Pediatric Speech Therapy Is

Speech therapy for children is care from a licensed speech-language pathologist (SLP), a professional trained to assess and support how children understand words, use words, make speech sounds, speak smoothly, and use communication with other people. It is not only about "talking clearly." A child who says words well but can't follow directions, or a child who understands everything but can't get words out, may both benefit.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) groups children's communication into a few broad areas. Knowing them helps you describe what you are seeing:

  • Receptive language: understanding what others say, such as following directions or pointing to pictures when named.
  • Expressive language: using words, phrases and sentences to share needs, ideas and stories.
  • Speech sounds (articulation and phonology): making sounds clearly enough for others to understand.
  • Fluency: the smooth flow of speech. Stuttering is a fluency difference.
  • Social communication: taking turns, sharing attention and using language with other people.
  • Voice and feeding: SLPs also work with voice quality and some swallowing and feeding concerns, usually with a physician involved.

Every child develops at their own pace. Milestones come in ranges, not fixed dates, and a child who is a little behind in one area may be right on track in others. Speech therapy is about understanding your child's strengths and needs, not putting a label on them.

Milestones

When to Seek Help: Speech and Language Milestones

The CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." program lists milestones that most children (about 3 out of 4) reach by a given age. If your child hasn't reached them yet, that doesn't mean something is wrong, but it is a good reason to talk with your pediatrician and consider a speech evaluation. Here is a short summary drawn from the CDC and ASHA:

Communication milestones most children reach by each age (CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early." and ASHA). Children vary; these are guides, not tests.
By aboutMost children can
12 monthsWave bye-bye, call a parent "mama," "dada" or another special name, and understand "no."
18 monthsTry to say three or more words besides "mama" or "dada," and follow one-step directions without gestures.
2 yearsSay at least two words together, like "more milk," and point to things in a book when asked.
30 monthsSay about 50 words and use two or more words with an action word, like "doggie run."
3 yearsHave short back-and-forth conversations, ask "who," "what," "where" or "why" questions, and be understood most of the time.
4 yearsSay sentences of four or more words and talk about something that happened during the day.
5 yearsTell a simple story with at least two events and keep a conversation going.

Signs worth acting on at any age

  • Your child loses words or skills they used to have.
  • Your child doesn't respond to their name or to sounds, or you wonder whether they hear well.
  • Strangers understand very little of what your preschooler says.
  • Your child repeats or stretches sounds, or gets "stuck," and seems frustrated or tense when talking.
  • Your child seems to understand much less than other children the same age.

Losing skills is always worth raising with your pediatrician promptly. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends developmental screening at well-child visits at 9, 18 and 30 months, plus autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months, so your pediatrician is a good first stop. A hearing check is often part of the next step, because even mild hearing loss can affect speech.

Areas of Care

What Speech Therapy Can Help With

Late talking and language delay

Toddlers who are slow to start talking or combining words, and preschoolers who struggle to understand or build sentences. Early support focuses on play and on coaching parents in everyday routines. See our speech delay guide.

Speech sound (articulation) concerns

Children who are hard to understand, leave sounds out, or have trouble with sounds like "r," "s" or "l," including lisps. Some sounds normally develop later than others, so an SLP looks at your child's age before recommending therapy. Read more about articulation differences in children.

Stuttering

Many young children go through a period of bumpy speech. An SLP can help you tell typical disfluency from stuttering that may need support, and can help your child talk with more ease and confidence. See stuttering therapy for kids.

Communication support for autistic children

Speech therapy can support communication for autistic children in ways that respect who they are, including spoken language, play, social communication and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) such as picture systems or speech-generating devices. Our SLPs do not diagnose autism; that is done by physicians and psychologists who specialize in it. Read speech therapy for autistic children.

Step by Step

How Speech Therapy Works at Our Practice

  • An in-person evaluation. A licensed speech-language pathologist talks with you about your child's history, plays with your child, and uses age-appropriate assessment tools. Learn what to expect at a speech evaluation.
  • Results in plain language. You'll hear what your child does well, what the SLP is concerned about, and whether therapy is recommended right now or whether to watch and recheck.
  • Clear, shared goals. Goals are written so you can see progress, such as "uses two-word phrases to request at snack time."
  • Play-based sessions with parent coaching. Young children learn through play. You'll be invited to watch or join, and you'll leave with a few simple things to try at home.
  • Coordination when it helps. With your permission, we can share information with your pediatrician, your child's school team or your Regional Center service coordinator, and with our own pediatric physical and occupational therapy team if your child has motor or sensory needs too.

How long therapy lasts depends on the child and the goals. Some children need a short block of sessions; others benefit from support over a longer period. Your SLP reviews progress with you regularly and adjusts the plan, and no one can promise a set timeline.

Research summarized by ASHA consistently shows that parents and caregivers matter a great deal. The words and routines you use between sessions, at bath time, in the car or at dinner, are where most practice happens. That's why we coach you, not just your child.

Honest Options

Free Options: California Early Start and School Services

We want you to know every route, including the free ones. Many families use public services, private therapy, or both.

Under 3: California Early Start

California Early Start is the state's early intervention program for babies and toddlers from birth to 36 months who have, or are at risk for, developmental delays. It is run through regional centers. In Stanislaus County, that is Valley Mountain Regional Center. You can refer your own child; you don't need a doctor's referral. If your child qualifies, services are written into an Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP).

Age 3 and up: your school district

From age 3, public school districts are responsible for evaluating children who may need special education, including speech and language services. You can ask your district for an assessment in writing. If your child qualifies, speech services are written into an Individualized Education Program (IEP) at no cost to you. School services focus on needs that affect your child's learning and participation at school.

How private therapy fits in

Private speech therapy can be an option while you wait for public services, if your child doesn't qualify for them, or alongside them for more practice. We compare the options honestly in private vs. school speech therapy.

Questions

Pediatric Speech Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

What age can a child start speech therapy?

There is no minimum age. Babies and toddlers can be evaluated when there are concerns, and early support for young children is usually play-based and centered on coaching parents.

Do I need a referral for speech therapy?

Call us and we'll explain what's needed for your situation. Some payment sources ask for a physician referral; as a physician-led practice, we can help coordinate that step.

How do I know if my child needs speech therapy?

Compare your child with the CDC and ASHA milestone ranges, and trust your instincts. If you're worried, an evaluation gives you a clear answer, and sometimes that answer is that your child is on track.

Is speech therapy free through the school or Regional Center?

For children who qualify, yes. California Early Start serves children under 3 through regional centers, and school districts provide speech services through an IEP from age 3. Eligibility rules apply.

How long does speech therapy take?

It varies with the child, the type of concern and how much practice happens at home. Your SLP will review progress with you regularly rather than promise a fixed number of sessions.
