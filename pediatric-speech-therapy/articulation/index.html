Speech Sounds

Articulation Disorders in Children: Sounds, Lisps and What Helps

An articulation disorder means a child has ongoing trouble making certain speech sounds past the age when most children have learned them. Some sounds, like r, s, l and th, normally come later, often around ages 5 to 7. An SLP's evaluation tells you whether your child's errors are expected for their age.

The Basics

What Is an Articulation Disorder?

ASHA uses the umbrella term speech sound disorders for ongoing difficulty saying sounds. It includes two main types:

  • Articulation disorders involve making individual sounds. A child might say "wabbit" for "rabbit" or "thun" for "sun."
  • Phonological disorders involve patterns that affect groups of sounds. For example, a child might replace all "back" sounds like k and g with front sounds like t and d ("tat" for "cat," "doe" for "go"), or drop the ends of words.

All young children make speech errors while they learn. These become a concern when they last past the age most children outgrow them, or when they make a child hard to understand. Less common causes, like childhood apraxia of speech (difficulty planning mouth movements) or hearing loss, need their own evaluation.

Age Ranges

When Do Children Learn Each Sound?

Children learn sounds over several years, in a broadly similar order. Ages vary between children and between studies, so treat these as approximate ranges based on ASHA guidance and published research on English speech development.

Approximate ages by which most children say English sounds clearly. Ranges vary between children and studies.
Approximate ageSounds most children have learned
About 2 to 3 yearsp, b, m, n, h, w, and often t, d
About 3 to 4 yearsk, g, f, y, ng
About 4 to 5 yearsl, s, z, sh, ch, j, v, and blends like "sp" or "bl" emerging
About 5 to 7 yearsr, th (as in "think" and "this"), and consistent clusters like "str" and "spl"

Intelligibility matters as much as single sounds. Strangers usually understand most of what a 4-year-old says, even if a few sounds are still developing. A 4-year-old who is hard for unfamiliar adults to understand is worth an evaluation.

Common Concerns

My Child Can't Say R or S: Trouble Sounds and Lisps

The "r" sound

"R" is one of the last sounds children learn, because it takes precise, hidden tongue positions. Saying "w" for "r" is common in preschoolers. If "r" errors continue past about age 7 or 8, they are less likely to resolve without help, and children may become self-conscious about them.

"S" sounds and lisps

A lisp is a common articulation difference that affects "s" and "z":

  • Frontal (interdental) lisp: the tongue pushes between the front teeth, so "sun" sounds like "thun." This is common in young children and many outgrow it.
  • Lateral lisp: air escapes over the sides of the tongue, giving a "slushy" sound. ASHA notes this type is not a typical part of development, so it's worth an evaluation at any age.

Do missing front teeth cause a lisp?

Losing baby teeth around ages 6 to 7 can briefly change "s" sounds. That usually settles when adult teeth come in. A lisp that started before tooth loss, or continues afterward, should be checked.

What about tongue-tie?

A tight frenulum under the tongue is sometimes blamed for speech errors. Research on tongue-tie and speech is mixed, and most children with mild tongue-tie develop clear speech. If you're concerned, an SLP can look at how your child's tongue moves for speech and, if needed, suggest you discuss it with your pediatrician.

Timing

When to Get Your Child's Speech Checked

Consider an evaluation if:

  • Your 3-year-old is understood by unfamiliar people less than about half the time.
  • Your 4-year-old is still hard for teachers or other adults to understand.
  • Your child leaves off the beginnings or ends of many words after about age 3 to 4.
  • Your child has a lateral (slushy) lisp at any age.
  • "R," "s" or "l" errors continue past the ages in the table above.
  • Your child seems frustrated, embarrassed or avoids talking because of how they sound.

Speech sound difficulties can also affect early reading and spelling, since both depend on hearing and working with the sounds in words. That's one reason not to wait too long.

Speech Therapy

How Articulation Therapy Works

A licensed speech-language pathologist starts by listening to your child say words and talk freely, and checks hearing history and how the mouth moves for speech. If therapy is recommended, it usually follows a path like this:

  1. Hearing and noticing the sound. Children learn to hear the difference between the correct sound and their version.
  2. Making the sound alone, with cues about where the tongue and lips go.
  3. Moving into syllables, words and phrases, through games and practice.
  4. Using the sound in conversation, at home and at school.

For phonological patterns, SLPs often work on contrasts, such as showing how "tea" and "key" mean different things, so children learn the pattern rather than one sound at a time. Short, regular home practice makes a big difference, and your SLP will give you a few simple activities.

How long does articulation therapy take?

It depends on how many sounds are affected, the type of difficulty, your child's age and attention, and how often practice happens at home. A child working on one later-developing sound may need a shorter course than a child with several phonological patterns or childhood apraxia of speech. Your SLP will track progress with you, adjust goals as sounds improve, and talk with you about when to step down or stop therapy. No one can promise a fixed number of sessions.

Questions

Articulation: Frequently Asked Questions

At what age should a child say the "r" sound?

Many children master "r" somewhere around ages 5 to 7. If errors continue past about 7 or 8, an evaluation is a good idea.

Will my child grow out of a lisp?

Many young children outgrow a frontal lisp. A lateral, slushy-sounding lisp is not a typical part of development and should be evaluated.

What is the difference between articulation and phonological disorders?

Articulation disorders involve making specific sounds. Phonological disorders involve patterns that affect groups of sounds, like replacing all k and g sounds with t and d.

Can I help my child practice sounds at home?

Yes. Model the correct word calmly ("Yes, a rabbit!") instead of correcting, and use the short practice activities your SLP gives you.
