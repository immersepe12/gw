Speech Delay

Is My Toddler's Speech Delayed?

A speech delay means a toddler is talking later than most children the same age. By 2, most toddlers use at least two words together; by 30 months, about 50 words. If your child isn't there yet, an evaluation can tell you whether to watch and wait or start support now.

Understanding the Terms

Speech Delay vs. Language Delay

Parents often use "speech delay" for any toddler who isn't talking much. Speech-language pathologists separate two ideas, because they call for different kinds of help:

  • Speech is the physical act of making sounds and words. A child with a speech delay may use plenty of words, but they're hard to understand.
  • Language is the system of words and rules we use to understand and share ideas. A child with a language delay may say words clearly but use few of them, or may not understand as much as other children the same age.

Language has two sides. Receptive language is understanding: following "get your shoes," pointing to the dog in a book. Expressive language is using words and gestures to communicate. A toddler who understands well but says little often has a different outlook than a toddler who also struggles to understand, which is one reason an evaluation looks at both.

How clear should my toddler's speech be?

A rule of thumb many SLPs use is that unfamiliar listeners understand roughly half of what a 2-year-old says, about three-quarters of what a 3-year-old says, and nearly all of what a 4-year-old says. Parents understand more because they know the context. If people outside the family understand very little of your 3-year-old's speech, mention it to your pediatrician.

Late Talkers

My 2-Year-Old Isn't Talking: Should I Wait or Act?

"Late talker" usually describes a toddler around 18 to 30 months who is behind on spoken words but developing typically in other ways: understanding language, playing, moving and connecting with people. ASHA's guidance on late language emergence often uses markers like fewer than 50 words or no two-word phrases at 24 months.

Here's the honest picture. Many late talkers catch up on their own by school age. But some don't, and research has not found a reliable way to tell which children will catch up just by looking at them at age 2. That's why "wait and see" is no longer the standard advice. An evaluation lets you "watch and help" instead, and support at this age is usually gentle, play-based and built around coaching you.

Signs that make an evaluation more urgent

  • Your child doesn't seem to understand simple words or directions.
  • Your child uses few gestures, such as pointing, waving or showing you things.
  • Your child doesn't babble much, or uses few different consonant sounds.
  • Your child has lost words or skills they used to have.
  • Your child doesn't respond to their name or to sounds.
  • There is a family history of speech, language or learning difficulties.
  • Your child seems frustrated by not being understood, with frequent meltdowns around communication.

Losing skills at any age, or concerns about hearing, should go to your pediatrician right away.

Why It Happens

What Can Cause a Speech or Language Delay?

Often there is no single clear cause. Some factors that clinicians consider include:

  • Hearing. Repeated ear infections with fluid, or hearing loss of any degree, can make it harder to learn sounds and words. A hearing test is a common early step.
  • Family history. Speech and language difficulties often run in families.
  • Premature birth or other health conditions that affect overall development.
  • Oral-motor differences, which affect planning and making mouth movements for speech. Childhood apraxia of speech is one example and needs an SLP's evaluation.
  • Broader developmental differences, including autism or intellectual disability. A speech delay alone does not mean either, and only a qualified physician or psychologist can make those diagnoses.

Do bilingual children talk later?

Learning two languages does not cause a language delay, according to ASHA. Bilingual toddlers may mix languages or have fewer words in each one, but their total vocabulary across both languages is usually similar to that of children learning one. Keep speaking the language you know best. Many families in Modesto and across the Central Valley raise children in English and Spanish, Punjabi or other languages, and that is a strength.

Does screen time cause speech delay?

Screens don't talk back. The AAP advises little or no screen use other than video chatting for children under about 18 to 24 months, and shared, limited viewing after that. Talking, reading and playing together give toddlers the back-and-forth practice they need.

Next Steps

What to Do If You're Worried

  1. Talk with your pediatrician. Bring notes on the words and gestures your child uses. The AAP recommends developmental screening at the 18- and 30-month visits and autism screening at 18 and 24 months.
  2. Ask about a hearing test. It rules out one important, treatable factor.
  3. Contact California Early Start if your child is under 3. Early Start is the state's free early intervention program for eligible infants and toddlers, run through regional centers. In Stanislaus County that's Valley Mountain Regional Center. You can make the referral yourself. Federal rules call for an evaluation and, if eligible, a service plan within 45 days of referral.
  4. Consider a private speech evaluation. Some families start privately while they wait for Early Start, if their child doesn't qualify, or to add practice. Learn what happens at a speech evaluation.

If your child also seems behind in crawling, walking or using their hands, read about motor delays in toddlers. Our pediatric therapy team can look at the whole picture.

Everyday Practice

Simple Ways to Help Your Toddler Talk at Home

  • Get face to face. Sit at your child's level so they can see your mouth and you can follow their interest.
  • Narrate the day. Use short phrases about what you're both doing: "Wash hands. Warm water. All clean."
  • Add one word. If your child says "ball," you say "big ball" or "throw ball."
  • Pause and wait. Give your child a few seconds to fill a gap before you jump in.
  • Offer choices. "Apple or banana?" invites a word without pressure.
  • Read and sing every day. Point to pictures, name them, and leave the last word of a familiar song for your child.

Try to avoid pressure like "say it" or "use your words." Praise any attempt, including gestures and sounds. These strategies help every toddler, but they don't replace an evaluation if you're concerned.

Speech Therapy

What Speech Therapy Looks Like for Toddlers

Toddler therapy looks like play, because that's how toddlers learn. A licensed speech-language pathologist sets up games, books and routines that create lots of chances to communicate, then coaches you in the same strategies so practice continues at home. Goals might include using more gestures, building a vocabulary of words for favorite things, or combining two words.

Progress depends on the child, the reason for the delay, and how much practice happens between sessions. Your SLP will review progress with you and adjust the plan. No one can promise a timeline, but support at this age is often a good use of a window when language is growing quickly.

Questions

Speech Delay: Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for a 2-year-old not to talk?

Most 2-year-olds say at least two words together, according to the CDC. A 2-year-old who isn't talking yet should be checked by a pediatrician and evaluated, even though many late talkers do catch up. Use milestones as a conversation starter with your pediatrician, not as a test.

Do late talkers catch up?

Many do, but not all, and it's hard to predict which children will. An evaluation helps you decide whether to monitor or start support. A toddler who understands well but says little often has a different outlook than one who also struggles to understand. That is why an evaluation looks at both.

Is speech delay a sign of autism?

Not on its own. Many children with speech delays are not autistic. If your pediatrician has broader concerns, they can refer you to a specialist who diagnoses autism. An SLP can still evaluate your child's speech and language and suggest ways to support communication.

Can my child get free help for a speech delay?

Children under 3 may qualify for California Early Start through their regional center, and children 3 and older may qualify for speech services through their school district. You can request either evaluation yourself. You can also ask your pediatrician which route fits your child's age.

What's the difference between a speech delay and a speech disorder?

A delay means skills are developing in the usual order but later than expected. A disorder means development is following a different pattern. An SLP's evaluation sorts this out. Speech is the physical act of making sounds and words, while language is how we understand and share ideas.
