Occupational Therapy

Sensory Processing and Pediatric OT in Modesto

Some children are unusually bothered by noise, textures or touch, or seek constant movement. Others struggle with pencils, buttons and scissors. Occupational therapy can help children build skills and coping strategies for daily life. Here is what parents notice, what the research honestly shows, and what therapy looks like.

The Basics

What Is Sensory Processing?

Sensory processing is how the brain takes in and makes sense of information from the senses: sight, sound, touch, taste and smell, plus the sense of body position and the sense of movement and balance. Most of us filter this information without thinking about it.

Some children respond to sensations much more strongly or much less strongly than other children. A child may cover their ears at a birthday party, melt down over sock seams, or seem to need to crash, spin and climb all day. When these responses get in the way of eating, dressing, sleeping, learning or play, parents often hear the term "sensory processing disorder."

It helps to know something honest up front: sensory processing disorder is not a recognized diagnosis in the DSM-5, the manual used to diagnose developmental and mental health conditions. In 2012 the American Academy of Pediatrics advised pediatricians not to use it as a stand-alone diagnosis, because sensory difficulties often occur alongside other conditions such as autism, ADHD, anxiety or developmental coordination disorder. The difficulties themselves are real, and an occupational therapist can still help with them.

For Parents

Signs Parents Commonly Notice

Every child has preferences. What matters is how strong the reactions are and how much they disrupt daily life. Parents often describe:

Over-responsive (sensations feel too strong)

  • Distress at loud or sudden sounds, like hand dryers or vacuums.
  • Strong dislike of tags, seams, haircuts, nail trims or tooth brushing.
  • A very narrow range of foods because of texture.
  • Avoiding messy play, swings or playground equipment.

Under-responsive or sensory seeking

  • Constant crashing, jumping, spinning or rough play.
  • Not seeming to notice pain, cold or a messy face.
  • Chewing on clothing or objects well past toddlerhood.
  • Seeming clumsy, slumping in a chair, or tiring quickly.

These signs can overlap with other developmental differences. If you also have concerns about speech, social connection or attention, talk to your pediatrician, who can arrange the right evaluations. We never diagnose autism or ADHD from sensory signs, and therapy does not make those conditions go away. If your child is autistic, our guide to speech therapy and communication support for autistic children may help too.

Honest Evidence

What the Research Says About Sensory Therapy

Research on sensory-based therapy is mixed, and we think parents deserve a straight answer:

  • Ayres Sensory Integration, a structured, play-based approach delivered by trained OTs, has the strongest support. A randomized trial by Schaaf and colleagues (2014) found autistic children who received it made more progress on their individual daily-living goals than a usual-care group. Studies are small, and results do not apply to every child.
  • Single sensory tools such as weighted vests or brushing on their own have weaker or inconsistent evidence.
  • The AAP (2012) recommended that families and pediatricians treat sensory therapy as one part of a broader plan, set clear goals, and check whether it is helping.

That is how we approach it: clear goals tied to daily life, regular progress checks with you, and an honest conversation if therapy isn't helping.

Hand Skills

Fine Motor Skills and Handwriting Help

Fine motor skills are the small movements of the hands and fingers. They grow over the preschool and early school years and depend on hand strength, core stability, coordination and the eyes and hands working together.

Every child develops at their own pace, but parents often ask about OT when a child:

  • Holds a crayon or pencil in a fist, or grips very tightly, well after peers have moved on.
  • Avoids drawing, puzzles, building or coloring.
  • Has handwriting that is very hard to read, very slow or makes the hand ache.
  • Has trouble with buttons, zippers, snaps or opening containers for their age.
  • Struggles to cut with scissors in kindergarten or later.

How OT helps with handwriting

An occupational therapist looks at what sits underneath the handwriting: posture, shoulder and hand strength, grip, letter formation, spacing and visual-motor skills. Practice is kept short and fun, using things like putty, tweezers games, chalkboards and multisensory letter practice. For some older children, the OT may also suggest keyboarding or other supports to discuss with the school.

At Our Practice

What Occupational Therapy Looks Like

  • An evaluation first. A licensed occupational therapist talks with you about daily routines, watches your child play and work, and may use parent questionnaires and standardized tests.
  • Goals about real life, such as getting through a haircut, eating a wider range of foods, or writing a sentence without hand pain.
  • Play-based sessions that build skills and help your child learn what helps them feel calm and ready.
  • Parent coaching on routines, sensory-friendly changes at home and ways to prepare for hard moments.
  • Coordination with speech therapy, pediatric PT or counseling within our practice when your child uses more than one service, and with school staff when you give permission.

Children 3 and older can also be evaluated for free by their school district under IDEA, and children under 3 through California Early Start (Valley Mountain Regional Center in Stanislaus County). Clinic OT can work alongside those services. Sometimes big feelings are part of the picture too; see counseling for children and teens.

Questions

Sensory Processing and Fine Motor: Frequently Asked Questions

Is sensory processing disorder a real diagnosis?

It isn't listed in the DSM-5, and the AAP advises against using it as a stand-alone diagnosis. The sensory difficulties children have are real, though, and occupational therapy can help with daily life.

Do sensory issues mean my child is autistic?

Not necessarily. Sensory differences are common in autism but also occur in many children who are not autistic. Only a qualified professional can diagnose autism, so talk to your pediatrician.

Does sensory integration therapy work?

Evidence is mixed. Structured Ayres Sensory Integration has some support from small trials in autistic children; other single techniques have weaker evidence. We set clear goals and check progress with you.

Can occupational therapy help with messy handwriting?

Often, yes. OTs work on grip, strength, posture, letter formation and visual-motor skills, and can suggest supports to discuss with your child's school.

Can my child get OT through school?

Yes, if an evaluation shows your child needs it to take part in school. Ask your district in writing for a free evaluation under IDEA.
