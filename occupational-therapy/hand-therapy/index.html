Occupational Therapy

Hand Therapy for Carpal Tunnel and Recovery

Hand therapy is occupational therapy focused on the hand, wrist and elbow. It can help with carpal tunnel symptoms, stiffness after a fracture and recovery after hand surgery, using splints, exercises and changes to daily tasks. At our Modesto practice, an evaluation by a licensed clinician comes first.

The Basics

What Is Hand Therapy?

Your hands do thousands of small jobs a day. When pain, numbness, stiffness or weakness gets in the way, simple tasks like opening a jar, typing, holding a phone or buttoning a shirt can become hard. Hand therapy is a focused type of rehabilitation for the hand, wrist, forearm and elbow, usually provided by an occupational therapist (and sometimes a physical therapist).

The aim is practical: less pain, better motion and strength, and getting back to the tasks that matter to you at home and at work. Common reasons people come in include:

  • Carpal tunnel symptoms: numbness, tingling or weakness in the thumb and fingers.
  • Stiffness and weakness after a wrist or hand fracture, once your doctor says it's time to move.
  • Recovery after hand surgery, such as carpal tunnel release or tendon repair, when your surgeon orders therapy.
  • Tendon problems such as trigger finger, de Quervain's tendinopathy or tennis elbow.
  • Arthritis in the hands, including help protecting joints during daily tasks.

Carpal Tunnel

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome and How Therapy Helps

Carpal tunnel syndrome happens when the median nerve is squeezed as it passes through a narrow tunnel in the wrist. Typical symptoms include numbness, tingling or burning in the thumb, index, middle and part of the ring finger, often worse at night or when holding a phone or steering wheel. Over time, some people notice weakness or dropping things.

Diagnosis comes from a physician, who may order nerve testing. For mild to moderate symptoms, guidelines from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons support non-surgical options as a first step. Hand therapy commonly includes:

  • Wrist splinting, usually at night, to keep the wrist in a neutral position.
  • Activity changes, such as adjusting grip, taking breaks and changing how you use tools or a keyboard.
  • Gentle exercises for the hand and wrist, guided by the therapist.
  • Workstation and task advice for jobs that involve repeated gripping or vibration.

Our physician can also review medical factors that sometimes go along with carpal tunnel symptoms, such as diabetes or thyroid conditions, and refer you to a hand surgeon if symptoms are severe or not improving. Tingling in the feet as well as the hands may point to a different problem; see our guide to neuropathy.

Rehab

Occupational Therapy After Hand Surgery or Injury

After a hand or wrist surgery or fracture, the hand can become stiff, swollen and weak. Your surgeon decides when therapy starts and what is safe at each stage, especially after tendon repairs, where moving too much too soon can cause harm. Therapy follows your surgeon's protocol and may include:

  • Swelling control and scar care once the incision has healed.
  • Range-of-motion exercises, starting gently and progressing as allowed.
  • Strengthening for grip and pinch when your surgeon clears it.
  • Practice with real tasks, such as dressing, cooking, writing and work tasks.
  • Splints when your surgeon or therapist recommends them.

Recovery time varies with the type of injury, the surgery and your overall health, so we don't promise a timeline. We send progress updates to your surgeon with your permission. If your recovery also involves the shoulder, knee or hip, see post-surgery rehab with physical therapy. For ongoing joint pain, see arthritis pain care.

Safety First

See a Doctor First If You Have These Signs

Some hand symptoms need medical attention before therapy. See a doctor promptly, or go to urgent care or the emergency room when it's severe, if you have:

  • A hand, finger or wrist that looks deformed, or pain after a fall or injury that could be a fracture.
  • A finger that is cold, pale, blue or numb after an injury.
  • Redness, warmth, swelling, pus or fever after surgery or a cut, which can signal infection.
  • Sudden weakness or numbness, especially with face drooping or trouble speaking. Call 911.
  • Constant numbness, or wasting of the muscle at the base of the thumb.

What Hand Therapy Visits Look Like

Your first visit is an evaluation with a licensed occupational therapist. They ask about your symptoms, work and daily tasks, review any surgery notes, and measure motion, grip strength, sensation and swelling. You'll leave with a plain-language explanation and a short home program. Follow-up visits check progress and adjust the plan.

Because we are one physician-led practice, your therapist can talk directly with our physician about medical questions and coordinate with physical therapy or pain management when needed. A physician referral or order may be required by your health plan, and your surgeon's orders guide care after surgery.

Questions

Hand Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Can carpal tunnel get better without surgery?

Mild to moderate symptoms often improve with non-surgical care such as night splinting and activity changes. Severe or worsening symptoms may need a surgeon's opinion. Therapy often includes night wrist splinting, activity changes, gentle exercises and workstation advice. Our physician can refer you to a hand surgeon if symptoms are severe or not improving.

Is hand therapy occupational or physical therapy?

It can be either. Most hand therapy is provided by occupational therapists, whose training focuses on hand function and daily tasks. Some physical therapists also provide it. Either way, the aim is less pain, better motion and strength, and a return to tasks at home and work.

When should I start therapy after hand surgery?

Your surgeon decides. Some surgeries call for therapy within days, while others need a period of protection first. Moving too much too soon after a tendon repair can cause harm, so your therapist follows your surgeon's orders closely at each stage of recovery.

Do I need a referral for hand therapy?

A physician referral or order may be required by your plan, and after surgery your surgeon's orders guide care. Call us and we'll explain the steps. Diagnosis of conditions such as carpal tunnel comes from a physician, who may order nerve testing.

Can hand therapy help arthritis in the hands?

It may. Hand therapy for arthritis often includes joint-protection tips, gentle exercises to keep motion, splints that rest painful joints, and easier ways to do daily tasks like opening jars or buttoning clothes. The aim is to reduce strain and keep you doing what matters to you. It does not change the arthritis itself, and results vary. A licensed therapist assesses your hands first.
