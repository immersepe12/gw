Occupational Therapy

Occupational Therapy for ADHD

Occupational therapy is not a treatment for ADHD itself. The core treatments are behavior therapy and, for many children, medication. But OT can help with the daily struggles that often come with ADHD, like messy handwriting, disorganized mornings, unfinished homework and self-care routines. Here is where it fits.

How ADHD Is Diagnosed and Treated

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common condition that affects attention, activity level and impulse control. The CDC estimates that about 1 in 9 U.S. children ages 3 to 17 have ever received an ADHD diagnosis, based on a 2022 national parent survey.

ADHD is diagnosed by a pediatrician, psychologist, psychiatrist or other qualified clinician, usually using information from parents and teachers about how a child acts in more than one setting. An occupational therapist does not diagnose ADHD, and we never suggest a diagnosis from what we see in therapy.

The American Academy of Pediatrics clinical practice guideline (2019) recommends:

  • Ages 4 to 5: parent training in behavior management and classroom behavior supports as the first step, with medication considered if problems remain significant.
  • Ages 6 to 11: FDA-approved medication together with parent training and/or behavioral classroom supports, plus school supports.
  • Ages 12 to 18: medication with the teen's agreement, and behavior therapy is encouraged too.

Medication decisions belong with your child's physician. Behavior supports and parent training are available through counseling; see ADHD counseling and counseling for children and teens.

Honest Answer

Where Occupational Therapy Fits

OT is a supporting service, not a replacement for the treatments above. Research on OT for ADHD specifically is limited, and studies of sensory-based approaches for ADHD symptoms have not shown clear benefit. We would rather tell you that up front.

Where OT can be useful is with the practical, everyday skills many children with ADHD find hard. Children with ADHD are more likely than other kids to also have motor coordination difficulties, and many struggle with the planning and organizing parts of daily life. An occupational therapist can help with:

  • Morning, bedtime and homework routines broken into clear, visual steps.
  • Organizing a backpack, desk or bedroom in ways that actually stick.
  • Handwriting legibility, speed and comfort.
  • Fine and gross motor coordination, such as using utensils, tying shoes or riding a bike.
  • Self-care, like dressing and grooming, without constant reminders.
  • Strategies for sitting through tasks, such as movement breaks and setting up a workspace.

Goals focus on function: what your child can do, not trying to make them sit still for its own sake.

Planning Skills

Executive Function in Kids: Routines That Work

Executive function is the brain's set of management skills: planning, starting tasks, remembering steps, keeping track of time and shifting between activities. These skills mature through childhood and into the twenties, and they often develop more slowly in children with ADHD.

Strategies OTs often use

  • Visual checklists with pictures or words for each step of getting ready.
  • Making time visible with timers or clocks that show time passing.
  • Breaking big tasks into small chunks, with a break or reward after each one.
  • A "launch pad" by the door for shoes, backpack and lunch.
  • Practicing the routine in sessions, then fading prompts as your child takes over.

These tools work best when the whole family uses them consistently. Your therapist will help you choose two or three that fit your household rather than overhauling everything at once.

School Skills

ADHD and Handwriting

Many parents of children with ADHD notice writing that is rushed, hard to read, poorly spaced or abandoned halfway through. Several things can be going on at once: attention drifting mid-word, rushing to finish, weak pencil control or a tiring grip.

An OT looks at each piece: posture, grip, letter formation, spacing, speed and how long your child can write before tiring. Practice uses short bursts, movement and multisensory methods. For some children, the therapist may suggest supports to raise with the school, like extra time, a slant board, graph paper for spacing or keyboarding.

For a fuller picture of grip and writing readiness, see fine motor skills by age.

At Our Practice

What OT Visits Look Like

  • An evaluation first. A licensed occupational therapist talks with you about daily routines, observes your child and may use questionnaires and standardized tests.
  • Specific goals, like getting dressed and out the door with fewer reminders or writing a readable paragraph.
  • Active, engaging sessions with parent coaching so strategies carry over at home.
  • Coordination with your child's physician and our counselors in a shared care plan, and with school staff when you give permission.

If ADHD affects learning, you can ask your school district in writing for a free evaluation for an IEP or a Section 504 plan. Questions

Occupational Therapy and ADHD: Frequently Asked Questions

Does occupational therapy treat ADHD?

Not directly. The AAP recommends behavior therapy and, for many children, medication as the main ADHD treatments. Occupational therapy can be a helpful addition for related struggles, such as handwriting, coordination, organization and self-care routines. It works best as one part of a plan that your child's physician and family shape together.

Can an occupational therapist diagnose ADHD?

No. ADHD is diagnosed by a pediatrician, psychologist, psychiatrist or other qualified clinician, using information from home and school. An occupational therapist can describe how attention and motor skills affect daily tasks, which may be useful to share with the person doing the evaluation, but OT findings are not a diagnosis.

Do weighted vests or fidgets help kids with ADHD?

Evidence for weighted vests and similar sensory tools in ADHD is weak and mixed. Some children find a fidget or movement break helpful for a particular task, and that is fine to try. An occupational therapist can help you test a strategy against a clear goal and drop it if it isn't making a real difference.

What are executive function skills in kids?

Executive function skills help children plan, start tasks, remember steps, manage time and switch between activities. They develop slowly through childhood and the teen years, often later in children with ADHD. Visual checklists, timers and simple, consistent routines can support them while they grow, and OT can help you set those up.

Can my child get help for ADHD at school?

Often, yes. You can request a free evaluation in writing from your school district. Depending on the results, your child may qualify for an IEP under IDEA or a Section 504 plan with classroom accommodations, such as extra time or preferential seating. Private OT and counseling can work alongside school supports.
