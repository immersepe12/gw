Headache and Migraine Care in Modesto

Frequent headaches and migraines can often be managed better with the right diagnosis, a plan for treating attacks early, prevention when headaches are frequent, and attention to sleep, stress and medicine overuse. Some headache warning signs need emergency care right away.

Generations Wellness Medical Practice · Medical director Dr. Rajwinder Singh Bahia, MD · Updated October 14, 2026

Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room for a sudden, severe "worst headache of your life," a headache with fever and stiff neck, confusion, weakness, numbness, trouble speaking or vision loss, or a headache after a head injury. Our practice is outpatient and does not treat emergencies.

The Basics

Common Types of Headache

Migraine

Migraine is a neurological condition, not "just a bad headache." Attacks usually last 4 to 72 hours and cause throbbing pain, often on one side, with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound. Some people have an aura first, such as flashing lights or zigzag lines. Migraine is about three times more common in women than men.

Tension-type headache

The most common headache. It feels like a tight band or pressure around the head, is usually mild to moderate, and is often linked to stress, poor sleep or neck and shoulder muscle tension.

Chronic daily headache

Headache on 15 or more days a month for more than three months. This can be chronic migraine, chronic tension-type headache, or headache made worse by frequent use of pain relievers.

Headaches from the neck

Problems in the upper neck can refer pain into the head. Physical therapy may help when the neck is involved. See neck pain physical therapy.

Patterns

Triggers and Medication Overuse Headache

Common migraine triggers include missed meals, poor or irregular sleep, stress (and the let-down after stress), dehydration, alcohol, hormonal changes, bright light and strong smells. Central Valley heat and summer smoke days can be a factor for some people. A headache diary of days, timing, possible triggers and medicines used is one of the most useful tools you can bring to a visit.

What to track in a headache diary

  • The date, time the headache started and how long it lasted
  • Where it hurt and how bad it was, from 0 to 10
  • Other symptoms, such as nausea, light sensitivity or aura
  • Sleep the night before, meals, fluids, caffeine and alcohol
  • Every medicine you took, the dose and whether it helped
  • For people who menstruate, where you were in your cycle

Two to three months of entries usually show a pattern that helps your clinician choose the right treatment and judge whether prevention is worth trying.

When pain relievers make headaches worse

Taking acute headache medicines too often can lead to medication overuse headache, sometimes called rebound headache. As a rough guide, this risk rises with simple pain relievers on 15 or more days a month, or triptans or combination products on 10 or more days a month. Opioid and butalbital-containing medicines carry a particular risk and are generally not recommended for migraine. If you are taking something for headaches most days, a physician can help you plan a safe change.

Treatment

Chronic Headache and Migraine Treatment

Treating an attack

Acute treatment works best taken early. Options a physician may discuss include NSAIDs, acetaminophen, anti-nausea medicines, and migraine-specific medicines such as triptans or newer gepants. The right choice depends on your heart health, other conditions and how your attacks behave.

Preventing attacks

The American Headache Society suggests considering preventive treatment when migraine occurs often or significantly disrupts life. Daily preventive medicines include certain blood pressure medicines, antidepressants and anti-seizure medicines; newer CGRP-targeting medicines are another option. Some preventive treatments, such as specific injections for chronic migraine, are given by specialists; we refer you if one may be right for you.

Lifestyle foundations

  • Regular sleep and wake times, including weekends
  • Regular meals and enough water
  • Regular aerobic exercise
  • Limiting caffeine and alcohol

Behavioral approaches

Relaxation training, biofeedback and cognitive behavioral therapy have research support for migraine and tension-type headache prevention. Counseling can also help when anxiety or stress drives headaches. See anxiety counseling.

Questions

Headaches and Migraines: Frequently Asked Questions

When should I see a doctor for headaches?

See a doctor if headaches happen several days a month, change pattern, disrupt work or sleep, or need pain relievers often. Emergency warning signs need 911 or an ER.

How do I know if it's a migraine?

Migraine is often throbbing, worse with activity, and comes with nausea or sensitivity to light and sound. A clinician makes the diagnosis from your history and exam.

Can taking pain relievers too often cause headaches?

Yes. Frequent use can lead to medication overuse headache. A physician can help you reduce use safely and start prevention.

Do you give Botox or nerve blocks for migraine?

No. If your physician thinks a procedure-based treatment may help, we refer you to a specialist.

Are migraines treated differently in pregnancy?

Yes. Some headache medicines are not safe in pregnancy. Talk with your obstetric clinician or physician before taking anything, and seek urgent care for a severe or unusual headache in pregnancy.
