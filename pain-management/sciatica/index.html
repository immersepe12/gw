Pain Management

Sciatica Treatment in Modesto

Sciatica is pain that runs from the low back or buttock down the leg, usually because a spinal nerve root is irritated. Most cases improve within a few weeks to a few months with activity, physical therapy and non-opioid medicines. Some warning signs need emergency care right away.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Rajwinder Singh Bahia, MD · October 24, 2026

Get emergency care now if leg pain comes with new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the groin or inner thighs, or weakness in both legs. These can be signs of cauda equina syndrome. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. Our practice is outpatient and does not treat emergencies.

The Basics

What Is Sciatica?

The sciatic nerve is the longest nerve in your body. It forms from nerve roots in your lower spine and runs through the buttock and down the back of each leg. "Sciatica" describes pain along that path. It usually affects one side.

Common symptoms

  • Pain that shoots or burns from the low back or buttock down the back of the leg, sometimes to the foot
  • Leg pain that is often worse than the back pain
  • Tingling, "pins and needles" or numbness in the leg or foot
  • Mild weakness in the leg or foot
  • Pain that gets worse with sitting, coughing or sneezing

What causes it

The most common cause is a herniated (bulging or slipped) disc in the lower spine pressing on or irritating a nerve root. Other causes include narrowing of the spinal canal (spinal stenosis), which is more common after age 60, bony growths from arthritis, and a vertebra that has slipped forward (spondylolisthesis). Less often, a tight piriformis muscle in the buttock may irritate the nerve. Rarely, an infection or tumor is the cause, which is why warning signs matter.

Risk goes up with age, long periods of sitting, heavy lifting or twisting at work, extra body weight and diabetes. Many farm, warehouse and trucking jobs common around Modesto and the Central Valley involve exactly these strains.

Sciatica or something else?

Not every pain in the buttock or leg is sciatica. Hip arthritis tends to cause groin pain and stiffness when you put on shoes or socks. Poor circulation in the leg arteries can cause calf cramping that comes on with walking and eases quickly with rest. Nerve damage from diabetes, called peripheral neuropathy, usually causes burning or numbness in both feet rather than pain shooting down one leg. A blood clot in the leg can cause calf swelling, warmth and redness and needs same-day medical care. Because these conditions are treated differently, an exam matters before you settle on a plan. Your clinician may check how far you can raise your straight leg, your reflexes, your strength and the feeling in your feet.

Recovery Time

How Long Does Sciatica Last?

For most people, the outlook is good. Acute sciatica often eases noticeably within about four to six weeks, and many people recover within three months without surgery. Disc material that presses on a nerve often shrinks over time on its own.

Recovery is not always a straight line. Pain can come and go, and some people have repeat episodes. When symptoms last longer than about three months, sciatica is considered chronic, and a more structured plan is usually needed. See also our guide to chronic back pain.

Treatment

Sciatica Treatment Options

Stay active

Bed rest is no longer recommended for sciatica. Gentle walking and normal activity, within comfort, usually lead to faster recovery than lying down for days.

Physical therapy

A licensed physical therapist can teach exercises that ease pressure on the nerve, strengthen your core and hip muscles, and improve how you sit, lift and bend. Many people find that certain positions or movements "centralize" the pain, moving it out of the leg and back toward the spine, which is often a good sign. Read about physical therapy for back pain.

Non-opioid medicines

Short-term use of NSAIDs such as ibuprofen or naproxen may ease pain for some people, though research shows only modest benefit for sciatica specifically. Nerve-pain medicines are sometimes considered, but studies of these for sciatica have shown mixed results. A physician weighs the benefits and side effects with you. Opioids are generally not a first choice. See non-opioid pain management.

Heat, ice and positioning

Heat or ice may ease muscle spasm. Lying on your back with a pillow under your knees, or on your side with a pillow between your knees, can take pressure off the nerve.

When a procedure or surgery may help

If severe pain continues despite several weeks of good treatment, or if nerve weakness is getting worse, your physician may order imaging such as an MRI and refer you to a spine specialist. Specialists may discuss options such as an epidural steroid injection or surgery to remove the part of the disc pressing on the nerve. We do not perform these procedures; we refer you and continue to coordinate your care.

Day to Day

What You Can Do at Home for Sciatic Nerve Pain

  • Take short walks several times a day rather than one long one.
  • Break up sitting every 20 to 30 minutes, especially when driving.
  • Lift with your legs and keep loads close to your body; avoid twisting while lifting.
  • Use heat or ice for 15 to 20 minutes at a time.
  • Avoid exercises that send pain further down the leg. Stop and ask a clinician if pain spreads.

Warning Signs

When to See a Doctor for Sciatica

Call 911 or go to an emergency room for loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the groin or "saddle" area, weakness in both legs, fever with back pain, or sciatica after a serious fall or crash.

Book a visit soon if:

  • Pain is severe and not easing after a week of self-care
  • You notice foot drop, tripping, or weakness getting worse
  • Symptoms last longer than four to six weeks
  • You have a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss or a weak immune system
  • Pain keeps coming back

Questions

Sciatica: Frequently Asked Questions

How long does sciatica usually last?

Many cases improve within four to six weeks, and most people recover within a few months without surgery. Pain lasting over three months is considered chronic and deserves a physician review.

Should I rest in bed with sciatica?

No. Staying gently active within comfort usually helps more than bed rest. Short walks and changing position often are good starting points.

Do I need an MRI for sciatica?

Usually not at first. Imaging is generally reserved for warning signs, worsening weakness, or symptoms that persist despite several weeks of treatment, or when surgery is being considered.

Can physical therapy help sciatica?

Physical therapy is a common first-line treatment. It focuses on movement, strength and posture habits that may ease nerve irritation and lower the chance of flare-ups.

Do you give epidural injections for sciatica?

No. If your physician thinks an injection may help, we refer you to a specialist who performs them.
