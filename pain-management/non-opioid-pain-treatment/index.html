Pain Management

Non-Opioid Pain Management: Options That Work

Non-opioid pain management uses exercise, physical and occupational therapy, non-opioid medicines, sleep and stress care, and counseling, combined into one plan. For many common chronic pain conditions, CDC guidance says these options work at least as well as opioids, with fewer serious risks.

The Evidence

Why Start With Non-Opioid Treatment?

For decades, opioids were widely prescribed for long-term pain. We now know more. Opioids can help with severe short-term pain, such as right after surgery. But for chronic pain, studies have not shown that they work better over the long run than other options, and they carry real risks: tolerance, dependence, constipation, drowsiness, falls, and overdose.

The CDC's 2022 guideline for prescribing opioids reviewed the research and concluded that non-opioid therapies are at least as effective as opioids for many common types of chronic pain, including low back pain, neck pain, osteoarthritis of the knee and hip, and fibromyalgia. The guideline recommends that clinicians use non-drug and non-opioid treatments as fully as possible first.

"Non-opioid first" is not the same as "no help." It means starting with the safest effective options and adding more only when the benefits clearly outweigh the risks for you.

Without Medicine

Non-Drug Treatments for Chronic Pain

Exercise and physical therapy

Movement is one of the best-studied treatments for chronic back pain, knee and hip arthritis, and fibromyalgia. Rest feels natural when something hurts, but too much rest leads to stiff joints and weak muscles, which can make pain worse. A licensed physical therapist builds a program that starts where you are, increases slowly, and teaches you how to handle flare-ups. Read about physical therapy in Modesto and physical therapy for back pain.

Occupational therapy

Occupational therapists help you do daily tasks with less strain: setting up a workstation, using tools that protect sore joints, pacing chores, and planning your day so pain does not run it. See occupational therapy.

Counseling and pain coping skills

Pain is processed in the brain, and stress, poor sleep and worry can turn up its volume. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for pain is one of the best-studied psychological approaches. It teaches skills to manage pain-related thoughts, pace activity and improve sleep. Counseling does not mean your pain is "in your head." It means your whole nervous system is part of the plan. Learn about counseling for chronic pain.

Sleep, weight and daily habits

Poor sleep makes pain feel worse, and pain makes sleep worse. Improving sleep habits can break that loop. For knee and hip arthritis, losing even a modest amount of weight reduces the load on joints. Staying active through walking, swimming or tai chi helps many people.

Other options with some evidence

Research reviewed by the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health suggests that some people with low back pain or arthritis may benefit from yoga, tai chi, massage, spinal manipulation or acupuncture. Results vary from person to person. These are not services we provide, but we are happy to talk through whether they may fit your plan.

Medication Options

Non-Opioid Medicines for Pain

Several types of non-opioid medicines are used for pain. Which one (if any) fits you depends on the type of pain, your other health conditions and the other medicines you take. A physician should guide these choices; even over-the-counter pain relievers have risks.

Common non-opioid medicine groups used for pain. General information only, not a recommendation.

NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, naproxen)Back pain, arthritis, headachesStomach bleeding, kidney strain, blood pressure and heart risks

Using an antidepressant or anti-seizure medicine for pain does not mean you are depressed or have seizures. These medicines can calm overactive pain signals in the nerves.

Putting It Together

What Is Multimodal Pain Care?

"Multimodal" simply means using more than one type of treatment at the same time. Chronic pain has physical, emotional and practical sides, so a plan that addresses each tends to work better than any single treatment alone. A typical multimodal plan might combine:

  • A medicine adjustment, such as switching to a topical NSAID or a nerve-pain medicine
  • A physical therapy program with home exercises
  • Occupational therapy for work or home tasks that trigger flare-ups
  • A few counseling sessions to build coping and sleep skills
  • A referral to a specialist if a procedure or further testing may help

Setting realistic goals

For most chronic pain, the goal is less pain and better function, not zero pain. Good goals are specific: walk for 20 minutes, sleep six hours, garden for an hour. Progress is often gradual and may include setbacks. Tracking function helps you and your clinicians see what is working.

Safety

If You Already Take an Opioid

Many people have taken an opioid for years, often as prescribed. If that is you, you are welcome here. Please do not stop an opioid suddenly. Stopping abruptly can cause withdrawal, worse pain and other risks. The CDC and FDA both advise that any change be gradual and planned with the prescriber.

Bring a full list of your medicines to your visit. We will look for interactions, talk about your goals and coordinate with the doctor who prescribes your opioid. If you or someone in your home takes opioids, ask a pharmacist about naloxone, a medicine that can counteract an opioid overdose in an emergency.

If you are struggling with opioid use, the SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) is free, confidential and open 24 hours.

Questions

Non-Opioid Pain Care: Frequently Asked Questions

Can chronic pain really be managed without opioids?

For many people, yes. CDC guidance found non-opioid treatments work at least as well as opioids for common conditions like low back pain and osteoarthritis. Results vary, and plans are built around your situation.

What is the strongest non-opioid pain reliever?

There isn't one best choice for everyone. The right medicine depends on the type of pain: NSAIDs for inflammation, nerve-pain medicines for neuropathy, and so on. A physician weighs benefits against your personal risks.

Is counseling for pain only for people with depression?

No. Pain coping skills help many people sleep better, pace activity and feel more in control, whether or not they have depression.

How long before a non-opioid plan helps?

It varies. Some medicines take several weeks to show their effect, and exercise programs build gradually. Your clinician reviews progress and adjusts the plan.

Do you offer injections as part of non-opioid care?

No. We refer you to a specialist when a procedure may help, and keep coordinating the rest of your plan.
