Pain Management

Arthritis Pain Treatment in Modesto

Arthritis pain, most often from osteoarthritis of the knees, hips and hands, is usually managed best with regular exercise, physical therapy, weight management and non-opioid medicines such as topical NSAIDs. A physician evaluation helps confirm the type of arthritis and when a specialist referral makes sense.

The Basics

What Causes Arthritis Pain?

Arthritis means joint inflammation, but the word covers more than 100 conditions. The CDC estimates that more than 53 million U.S. adults have been diagnosed with some form of arthritis.

Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis is the most common type. The cartilage that cushions the ends of bones wears down over time, and the whole joint changes. It most often affects the knees, hips, hands and spine. Symptoms include pain with use, stiffness after rest that eases within about 30 minutes, swelling and reduced motion. Age, past joint injuries, extra body weight and jobs with heavy kneeling or lifting raise the risk.

Inflammatory arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis and gout are driven by the immune system or by crystals in the joint. Warning signs include morning stiffness lasting more than an hour, swelling in several joints on both sides of the body, or sudden hot, red, very painful joints. These types need specific medical treatment, often from a rheumatologist, and early diagnosis matters.

Treatment

Treatment for Arthritis and Knee Osteoarthritis Pain

Guidelines from the American College of Rheumatology and the Arthritis Foundation put exercise and self-management at the center of osteoarthritis care, with medicines added as needed.

Exercise and physical therapy

Strengthening the muscles around a joint takes load off it. Walking, cycling, water exercise and tai chi are all options. A licensed physical therapist can design a program that works for your joints and help you push through the early weeks, when exercise can feel uncomfortable. See physical therapy for knee pain.

Weight management

For knee and hip arthritis, extra body weight adds force with every step. Losing even 5% to 10% of body weight can ease pain and improve function for many people with knee osteoarthritis.

Protecting joints in daily life

Occupational therapists help with hand arthritis and daily tasks: splints, easier-grip tools, and ways to cook, garden or work with less strain. See hand therapy.

Non-opioid medicines

  • Topical NSAIDs (gels applied to the skin) are often recommended first for knee and hand osteoarthritis, with fewer whole-body side effects than pills.
  • Oral NSAIDs may help, but carry stomach, kidney and heart risks, especially in older adults.
  • Acetaminophen offers modest benefit for some people and has liver risks at high doses.
  • Duloxetine is sometimes used for osteoarthritis pain when other options are not enough.

A physician reviews these against your health and other medicines. Read more about non-opioid pain management.

Heat, cold and aids

Warm showers ease stiffness; ice can calm a swollen joint after activity. A cane in the hand opposite a painful knee or hip, or supportive shoes, can make walking easier.

Injections and joint replacement

When arthritis remains severe despite these steps, joint injections or joint replacement surgery may be options. We do not perform these. If your physician thinks a procedure may help, we refer you to an orthopedic or other specialist, and our physical therapists can support you before and after surgery. See post-surgery rehab.

Day to Day

Living Well With Arthritis

Arthritis is usually a long-term condition, so the habits you build matter as much as any single treatment. The CDC recommends that adults with arthritis aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, broken into short sessions if needed, plus muscle-strengthening work twice a week. Start lower and build up. Even 10 minutes at a time counts.

Handling flare-ups

Most people with osteoarthritis have good days and bad days. During a flare, cut back activity rather than stopping it, use heat or ice, and return to your usual routine as the joint settles. If a flare lasts more than a week or two, or the joint becomes hot and red, contact a clinician.

Sleep and mood

Ongoing joint pain can disturb sleep and wear down your mood, which in turn makes pain harder to cope with. Talking about this openly with your clinician is part of good arthritis care. Counseling for living with chronic pain can help with sleep, pacing and stress.

Self-management programs

The CDC lists proven, low-cost programs that teach arthritis self-management and safe exercise. Many are offered through community centers and online. Ask us which may fit you.

Warning Signs

When to See a Doctor for Joint Pain

Get same-day or emergency care for a joint that is suddenly hot, red and swollen, especially with fever (this can be infection), or a joint that is deformed or can't bear weight after an injury.

Book a visit if joint pain lasts more than a few weeks, keeps you from usual activities, wakes you at night, involves several joints, or comes with long morning stiffness, rash or fatigue.

Questions

Arthritis Pain: Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best treatment for knee osteoarthritis?

Exercise and strengthening are the foundation, often combined with weight management and topical NSAIDs. The right mix depends on your health and how severe the arthritis is. Our clinicians focus on non-opioid options first. They review your health and goals at your first visit to build a plan that fits you.

Should I rest an arthritic joint?

Short rest during a flare can help, but long-term inactivity tends to make joints stiffer and weaker. Regular, gentle activity is usually better. Gentle movement helps keep joints flexible and muscles strong. Exercise and strengthening are a key part of arthritis care. Your care team can suggest safe ways to stay active during a flare.

What kind of doctor treats joint pain?

Primary care physicians manage most osteoarthritis. Rheumatologists treat inflammatory arthritis, and orthopedic surgeons handle joint replacement. Each type of specialist plays a different role. Your primary care physician can help you decide who to see and when. If you are not sure where to start, our team can talk through your options with you and help you plan next steps.

Do you give cortisone shots for arthritis?

No. If your physician thinks a joint injection may help, we refer you to a specialist. Our team focuses on non-opioid options such as exercise and strengthening. We will talk with you about whether an injection could help and who to see. Your care plan depends on your health and how severe the arthritis is. Call (369) 222-0979 or (209) 809-0022 with questions.

Why does arthritis pain get worse at night?

Many people notice more joint pain at night or first thing in the morning. Joints can stiffen during long periods without movement, and with fewer distractions at night, pain may feel stronger. Gentle movement before bed, a warm shower and a supportive sleep position may help. Pain that wakes you every night, or comes with fever or a hot, swollen joint, should be checked by a physician.
