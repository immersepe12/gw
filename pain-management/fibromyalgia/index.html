Fibromyalgia Care in Modesto

Fibromyalgia causes widespread pain, tiredness, poor sleep and trouble focusing. It is a real condition involving how the nervous system processes pain. The best-supported treatment plans combine gentle, gradual exercise, sleep care, pain coping skills and, for some people, non-opioid medicines.

The Basics

What Is Fibromyalgia?

Fibromyalgia is a long-term condition with pain in many areas of the body, usually along with fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, and problems with memory or focus that people often call "fibro fog." The CDC estimates it affects about 4 million U.S. adults, roughly 2% of the adult population, and it is more common in women.

Fibromyalgia does not damage joints or muscles. Research suggests the brain and spinal cord process pain signals differently, turning up the volume on pain. That is why it can be hard to see on tests, and why people with fibromyalgia are sometimes told nothing is wrong. The pain is real.

Common symptoms

  • Widespread aching or burning pain, on both sides of the body, above and below the waist
  • Fatigue and waking up tired
  • Trouble thinking clearly or remembering
  • Headaches, irritable bowel symptoms, and sensitivity to noise, light or temperature
  • Anxiety or low mood, which often travel with long-term pain

Symptoms often flare with stress, poor sleep, illness or overdoing activity.

Getting Answers

How Fibromyalgia Is Diagnosed

There is no single test for fibromyalgia. A physician diagnoses it based on your symptoms: widespread pain lasting at least three months along with fatigue, sleep and thinking problems, after considering other explanations. Blood tests are often used to rule out conditions that can look similar, such as low thyroid, anemia, vitamin D deficiency or inflammatory arthritis.

Fibromyalgia can also exist alongside other conditions like osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis. A careful evaluation helps make sure each problem gets the right care. Bring any past lab results and a list of treatments you have already tried to your first visit.

Treatment

What a Fibromyalgia Treatment Plan Includes

European (EULAR) recommendations for fibromyalgia put non-drug treatments first, especially exercise, with medicines added for some people. Most plans combine several pieces:

Gradual exercise

Exercise is the treatment with the strongest evidence in fibromyalgia. The key word is gradual: starting with a few minutes of walking, water exercise or gentle strengthening, then building slowly. Doing too much on a good day can trigger a flare. A licensed physical therapist can help you find a starting point and pace. See physical therapy.

Sleep

Better sleep often means less pain. Regular sleep and wake times, limiting caffeine and screens late in the day, and checking for sleep apnea can all help.

Pain coping skills and counseling

Cognitive behavioral therapy for pain and insomnia can help people manage flares, pace activity and cope with stress. This is not a claim that fibromyalgia is psychological; it is a way to calm a sensitive nervous system. Learn about counseling for chronic pain.

Pacing daily life

Occupational therapy can help you plan your day, break tasks into smaller steps and save energy for what matters most. See occupational therapy.

Non-opioid medicines

Three medicines have been approved by the FDA for fibromyalgia: duloxetine, milnacipran and pregabalin. Low-dose amitriptyline is also commonly used. These help some people with pain and sleep, with side effects such as drowsiness, dizziness or nausea. Opioids are not recommended for fibromyalgia; they have not been shown to help and carry real risks. A physician reviews options with you. See non-opioid pain management.

Managing flares

Flares are part of fibromyalgia for most people, so it helps to have a plan before one starts. Many people keep a simple diary of sleep, activity, stress and pain to spot their own triggers. During a flare, scale activity back rather than stopping completely, use heat, gentle stretching or a warm bath, protect your sleep, and lean on the coping skills you have practiced. Once the flare settles, return to your usual routine step by step. If a flare comes with new symptoms such as fever, swollen joints or weakness, contact a clinician, since these are not typical of fibromyalgia.

What about supplements and other therapies?

Many products are marketed for fibromyalgia. Most have little good evidence behind them, and some interact with prescription medicines. Tai chi, yoga and water-based exercise have some supportive research. Talk with your clinician before starting supplements, and be cautious of anything that promises to end fibromyalgia.

Questions

Fibromyalgia: Frequently Asked Questions

What kind of doctor treats fibromyalgia?

Primary care and internal medicine physicians manage most fibromyalgia, often with physical therapists and counselors. Rheumatologists help when another joint or immune condition is suspected.

Is fibromyalgia real?

Yes. It is a recognized condition linked to changes in how the nervous system processes pain, even though routine tests are often normal.

Does exercise make fibromyalgia worse?

Too much too fast can trigger a flare. Starting small and building slowly is one of the best-supported ways to reduce symptoms over time.

Can fibromyalgia go away?

It is usually long-term, but many people find symptoms become more manageable with a consistent plan. Symptoms can vary over time.
