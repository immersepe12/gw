Pain Management

Chronic Back Pain Care in Modesto

Chronic back pain is back pain that lasts longer than about three months. Most of it is not caused by serious disease, and it often responds best to a combined plan: exercise and physical therapy, non-opioid medicines where appropriate, and support for sleep and stress, guided by a physician.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Rajwinder Singh Bahia, MD · October 24, 2026

Get emergency care for back pain with new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the groin, sudden leg weakness, fever, or pain after a major fall or crash. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. Our practice is outpatient and does not treat emergencies.

The Basics

What Is Chronic Back Pain?

Low back pain is one of the most common reasons adults see a doctor. Most episodes get better within a few weeks. When pain lasts longer than about 12 weeks, it is called chronic. Chronic low back pain is a leading cause of disability worldwide, and it often affects work, sleep and mood.

Common causes

  • Non-specific (mechanical) back pain — the most common type, involving muscles, ligaments, joints and discs without one clear structural cause
  • Disc problems — wear-related changes or a herniated disc, which can also cause sciatica
  • Spinal stenosis — narrowing of the spinal canal, often causing leg pain with walking that eases when you sit or bend forward
  • Arthritis of the spine — wear in the small facet joints
  • Less common causes — inflammatory conditions, fractures from osteoporosis, infection or cancer

Why imaging doesn't always explain the pain

Many adults with no back pain at all have disc bulges and "degeneration" on an MRI. These findings are often a normal part of aging. That is why guidelines advise against routine imaging for back pain without warning signs: a scan can find changes that are not the cause, and lead to worry or treatments that don't help.

Why pain can persist after tissues heal

In chronic pain, the nervous system can become more sensitive, so pain signals keep firing even after the original strain has healed. Stress, poor sleep, fear of movement and inactivity can all feed that cycle. This does not mean the pain is imagined. It means treatment works best when it addresses the body and the nervous system together.

Common Question

When Should You See a Pain Doctor for Back Pain?

It is reasonable to see a physician about back pain when:

  • Pain has lasted longer than six weeks despite self-care, or longer than three months at any level
  • Pain wakes you at night or does not ease with rest
  • Pain spreads below the knee, or you have numbness, tingling or weakness in a leg
  • You are over 50 with new back pain, or have a history of cancer, osteoporosis or steroid use
  • You have unexplained weight loss or fevers
  • Pain is keeping you from work, sleep or the things you care about
  • You are relying on pain medicines more and more and want a safer plan

A primary care doctor can manage much back pain. A pain-focused physician can help when pain has become long-term, when several treatments have not worked, or when you need a coordinated plan with therapy and specialist input.

Your Visit

What a Back Pain Evaluation Involves

  1. Your story. When the pain started, what makes it better or worse, what you have tried, and how it affects daily life.
  2. Screening for warning signs. Questions about bladder and bowel function, fevers, weight loss and past medical history.
  3. A physical exam. How you move, your strength, reflexes and sensation in your legs.
  4. Medicine review. Everything you take, including over-the-counter pain relievers and supplements.
  5. Decisions about testing. Imaging or blood tests only when the history and exam suggest they will change the plan.
  6. A shared plan. Clear goals, first steps, and any referrals.

Bring past imaging reports, notes from other doctors and a list of treatments you have tried. It saves time and avoids repeating tests.

Treatment

Chronic Back Pain Treatment That Helps

The American College of Physicians' guideline for low back pain recommends starting with non-drug treatments for chronic back pain. The CDC's 2022 opioid guideline likewise finds non-opioid options at least as effective as opioids for low back pain.

Exercise and physical therapy

Exercise therapy is a cornerstone of chronic back pain care. A licensed physical therapist can guide strengthening, stretching and gradual return to activities, and help you overcome fear of movement. See physical therapy for back pain.

Non-opioid medicines

NSAIDs may help some people for limited periods. Certain antidepressants such as duloxetine are also used for chronic back pain. A physician weighs each option against your heart, kidney and stomach health and your other medicines. Read our guide to non-opioid pain management.

Counseling and pain coping skills

Cognitive behavioral therapy and similar approaches help people manage pain-related stress, sleep and activity pacing, and are recommended in back pain guidelines. Learn about counseling for chronic pain.

Work and daily-life changes

Occupational therapy can help you adjust lifting, desk setup or driving habits so daily tasks are less likely to trigger flare-ups. See occupational therapy.

Sleep, weight and staying at work

Small changes add up. A steady sleep schedule, gradual weight loss if needed, and quitting smoking can all support recovery. Research also suggests that staying at work, with adjusted duties if needed, often leads to better outcomes than long stretches off. Your clinician can help you plan a safe return.

Procedures and surgery

A small share of people with chronic back pain may benefit from an interventional procedure or surgery, usually when there is a clear structural cause such as nerve compression. We do not perform these procedures. When your physician thinks one may help, we refer you to a specialist and keep coordinating the rest of your plan.

Questions

Chronic Back Pain: Frequently Asked Questions

What kind of doctor is best for chronic back pain?

Many people start with a primary care or pain-focused physician who can evaluate the cause and coordinate therapy. Spine surgeons and interventional specialists are usually involved when there is a clear structural problem or warning sign. Our practice brings evaluation, non-opioid care, therapy and counseling together under one shared plan.

Do I need an MRI for chronic back pain?

Not always. Imaging is generally recommended for warning signs, nerve problems that are getting worse, or when a procedure or surgery is being considered. Without warning signs, a scan can find changes that are not the cause of pain. That can lead to worry or treatments that don't help. A clinician will check for warning signs at your evaluation and decide whether a scan makes sense.

Will chronic back pain ever go away?

Many people see meaningful improvement in pain and function with a consistent plan, though some have ongoing or recurring symptoms. Goals focus on doing more with less pain. A combined plan often works best, with exercise and physical therapy, non-opioid medicines where appropriate, and support for sleep and stress. Your physician reviews progress and adjusts the plan with you over time.

Is walking good for chronic back pain?

For many people, yes. Regular walking, built up gradually, is a simple way to stay active. A physical therapist can tailor the pace to you. Exercise and physical therapy are a core part of most plans for chronic back pain. Before starting, a licensed clinician should check your back for warning signs. That way, activity can be matched to the likely cause of your pain.

Do you offer back injections?

No. When an injection or other procedure may help, we refer you to a specialist. Our physician-led practice focuses on evaluation, non-opioid medication management, physical therapy, occupational therapy and counseling. We coordinate with the specialist so your care stays connected under one shared plan.
