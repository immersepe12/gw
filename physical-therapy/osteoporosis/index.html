Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis Exercises and Physical Therapy

Osteoporosis makes bones thinner and more likely to break, but the right exercise still helps. A physical therapist can show you how to build strength, load your bones safely, protect your spine during daily tasks and lower your risk of falling, working alongside your doctor's plan for testing and medicine.

Safety First

Bone Symptoms That Need a Doctor First

Exercise is safe and helpful for most people with low bone density. See a physician before starting, or get urgent care, if you have:

  • Sudden, sharp back pain after a minor strain, cough or lift, which can signal a spine compression fracture
  • Pain in the hip or groin after a fall, or you cannot bear weight
  • A noticeable loss of height or a new forward curve in the upper back
  • Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, or new numbness or weakness in the legs

A physician decides whether you need a bone density (DEXA) scan, blood tests or medicine. Our physician can see you before therapy if needed.

The Basics

What Osteoporosis Means for Your Bones

Bone is living tissue that is always being broken down and rebuilt. With osteoporosis, more bone is lost than replaced, so bones become less dense and more fragile. Many people don't know they have it until a bone breaks, often in the wrist, hip or spine. The Bone Health & Osteoporosis Foundation notes that about half of women and up to one in four men over 50 will break a bone because of osteoporosis.

Risk goes up after menopause and with age, a small body frame, family history, smoking, heavy alcohol use, low calcium or vitamin D, long-term steroid use and some medical conditions. Osteopenia means bone density is lower than normal but not yet in the osteoporosis range; exercise helps there too.

Testing and medicine are your doctor's job

A DEXA scan measures bone density and gives a T-score. Your physician uses it, along with your fracture risk, to decide whether medicine makes sense and which kind. Many effective osteoporosis medicines exist. Exercise works alongside them, not instead of them.

Building Strength

Exercise for Bone Density: What Helps

Bones respond to load, much like muscles do. The NIH's National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases recommends weight-bearing and resistance exercise for bone health. The goal is to give bones a strong enough signal, safely, and to build the muscle and balance that prevent falls.

Strength training

Progressive resistance exercise, such as squats, step-ups, rows, wall push-ups and work with bands or weights, a few times a week. Research such as the LIFTMOR trial suggests that well-supervised, progressive strength training can be done by many people with low bone mass. Load is built up gradually and as tolerated, with good form first.

Impact as tolerated

Weight-bearing activity like brisk walking, stair climbing and, for some people, gentle heel drops or small hops adds a useful jolt to the hips and legs. How much impact is right depends on your fracture history, joints and balance, so your therapist helps you choose.

Posture and back-extensor strength

Exercises that strengthen the muscles along your spine and upper back help you stand taller and may lower the load on the front of the vertebrae.

Balance training

Most hip and wrist fractures happen in a fall. Balance practice, like standing on one leg near a counter, heel-to-toe walking and tai chi, is a key part of safe exercises for osteoporosis. Learn more on our balance and fall prevention page.

Protect Your Spine

Safe Exercises for Osteoporosis: Moving Without Straining the Spine

The spine's vertebrae are a common fracture site. Loaded forward bending, especially with twisting, puts the most pressure on the front of these bones. You don't need to fear movement, but it helps to change a few habits:

  • Hinge at the hips, not the waist. Bend your knees and keep your back long when you pick something up, load the dishwasher or garden.
  • Skip loaded flexion exercises such as sit-ups, crunches and toe-touch stretches, and avoid deep twists while holding weight.
  • Carry close. Hold bags and laundry baskets near your body.
  • Ask before yoga or Pilates. Many poses are fine, but some forward folds and spinal twists may need changes.
  • Make your home safer. Clear clutter, add lighting and grab bars, and use a non-slip bath mat.

If you already have back pain, see our back pain physical therapy page. For aching in several joints, our physician-led joint pain page explains common causes.

Questions

Osteoporosis Exercises: Frequently Asked Questions

Can exercise rebuild bone with osteoporosis?

Exercise can help slow bone loss and, in some studies, modestly improve bone density at the hip or spine. Just as important, it builds muscle strength and balance, which lowers the chance of falling and breaking a bone. Exercise works best alongside the calcium, vitamin D and medicine plan your physician recommends.

What exercises should I avoid with osteoporosis?

Avoid sit-ups, crunches, toe-touch stretches and twisting while holding weight, because loaded forward bending strains the front of the spine. High-impact moves may not suit everyone, especially after a fracture. A physical therapist can show you safer ways to do the movements you need for daily life and exercise you enjoy.

Is walking enough for osteoporosis?

Walking is a good start and helps the hips and heart, but on its own it may not give bones a strong enough signal. Most experts recommend adding progressive strength training and balance practice a few times a week. Your therapist can help you add these safely, at a level that suits you.

Can I lift weights if I have osteoporosis?

Many people can, with good form and a gradual build-up. Supervised, progressive strength training has been studied in people with low bone mass. Technique matters, especially keeping your back long and hinging at the hips. Ask your physician first if you have had a recent fracture, then work with a therapist on a plan.

Do I need a DEXA scan before starting physical therapy?

Not necessarily. You can begin safe strength and balance work without one. A physician decides when a DEXA scan is due based on your age and risk factors, and the results help guide medicine decisions. If you have had a scan, bring the results so your therapist can tailor the plan.
