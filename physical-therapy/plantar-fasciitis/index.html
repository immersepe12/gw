Plantar Fasciitis

Plantar Fasciitis Physical Therapy in Modesto

Plantar fasciitis is one of the most common causes of heel pain, and most people get better without surgery. Physical therapy uses stretching, strengthening, footwear advice and short-term tools like taping to ease pain on those first morning steps and help you stay on your feet with less discomfort.

Heel Pain

What Is Plantar Fasciitis?

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue that runs along the bottom of your foot from the heel to the toes. When it gets overloaded, the area where it attaches to the heel becomes painful. Many clinicians now call it plantar heel pain, because it often involves tissue thickening rather than true inflammation.

The classic sign is sharp pain under the heel with your first steps in the morning or after sitting, which eases a bit as you walk and then returns after long standing. Common contributing factors include:

  • A sudden increase in walking, running or standing
  • Long shifts on hard floors, common in warehouse, retail and farm work
  • Tight calf muscles or limited ankle bend
  • Higher body weight
  • Worn-out or very flat, unsupportive shoes

Safety First

Heel Pain That Needs a Doctor First

See a physician before therapy, or get urgent care, if you have:

  • Heel pain after a fall or jump, or pain that makes it impossible to put weight on the foot
  • Swelling, redness and warmth with fever
  • Numbness, tingling or burning in the foot, which can point to a nerve problem
  • Diabetes with any foot wound, color change or loss of feeling; see our wound care page
  • Pain at night or at rest that does not change with activity

Evidence-Based Care

What Plantar Fasciitis Physical Therapy Involves

The 2023 Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy (JOSPT) clinical practice guideline on heel pain recommends a mix of treatments rather than one single fix. Your physical therapist may use:

Stretching

Calf stretches and plantar fascia stretches, such as pulling your toes back before you get out of bed, help many people. They are often most useful first thing in the morning and after sitting.

Strengthening

Progressive strengthening of the calf and foot muscles, such as slow heel raises, helps the foot handle load again. Your therapist sets the starting level and builds it over weeks.

Manual therapy

Hands-on joint and soft tissue techniques can ease pain and stiffness, alongside exercise.

Taping and orthotics

Taping can give short-term relief so you can stay active. Shoe inserts, either ready-made or custom, may help some people in the short term.

Night splints

A night splint holds the ankle gently bent upward while you sleep. The guideline suggests it for people whose morning pain stays stubborn.

Education and load management

Your therapist helps you adjust standing time, running mileage or work tasks for a while, rather than stopping everything.

At Home

Plantar Fasciitis Exercises and Self-Care

These are common starting points. Ask your therapist which fit you and how many to do.

  • Calf stretch against a wall, back knee straight, then slightly bent, holding about 30 seconds
  • Toe-pull stretch before standing in the morning
  • Heel raises, slow up and slower down, progressing to one leg
  • Rolling the arch over a ball or frozen water bottle for comfort
  • Supportive shoes with cushioning, worn around the house instead of going barefoot on hard floors

An honest timeline

Plantar fasciitis often improves slowly. Many people notice progress over several weeks to a few months of consistent care, and some take longer. Most people recover without surgery. Your therapist tracks your progress and refers you back to a physician if things are not moving forward. For tendon pain at the back of the heel, see Achilles tendinitis.

Questions

Plantar Fasciitis Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

How long does plantar fasciitis take to heal?

It varies. Many people notice gradual improvement over several weeks to a few months with consistent stretching, strengthening and load changes, and a smaller group takes longer. Most people get better without surgery. Your physical therapist tracks progress with you and refers you to a physician if your heel pain is not improving as expected.

What are the best plantar fasciitis exercises?

Common choices are calf stretches, a toe-pull stretch before your first morning steps, and slow heel raises that build calf and foot strength. Rolling the arch over a ball can feel good. The right mix and amount depend on how irritable your heel is, so a physical therapist can tailor them and progress them over time.

Do night splints help plantar fasciitis?

They help some people. A night splint keeps the ankle gently bent upward while you sleep, so the calf and plantar fascia stay lengthened. The JOSPT heel pain guideline suggests it mainly for people whose morning pain lasts. It can feel awkward at first. Your therapist can tell you whether one makes sense for you.

Should I stop walking with plantar fasciitis?

Usually not completely. Total rest can weaken the foot and calf. Instead, reduce long standing or high mileage for a while, wear supportive shoes, and keep up your exercises. Lower-impact options like cycling or swimming can keep you fit. Your therapist will help you find a level of activity your heel tolerates.

Do I need a referral for plantar fasciitis PT?

Not to begin. California's direct access law lets you see a physical therapist without a referral for up to 45 days or 12 visits, whichever comes first. A physician or other qualified provider then needs to sign the plan to continue. Insurance plans may have their own rules, so it helps to check with yours.
