Vertigo

Vertigo and Vestibular Therapy in Modesto

Vertigo is a spinning or tilting feeling that can make everyday life hard. Many common causes, such as BPPV and inner ear inflammation, respond well to vestibular physical therapy. A licensed physical therapist uses repositioning maneuvers and targeted exercises to help your brain and inner ear work together again and reduce dizziness.

Emergency First

Dizziness That Needs 911

Sudden dizziness can sometimes be a sign of stroke. Call 911 right away if dizziness or loss of balance comes on suddenly with any of these BE FAST warning signs:

  • Balance: sudden loss of balance or coordination
  • Eyes: sudden vision loss or double vision
  • Face: one side of the face droops
  • Arms: weakness or numbness in one arm or leg
  • Speech: slurred or confused speech
  • Time: note the time and call 911 now

Also get emergency care for vertigo with a sudden severe headache, fainting, chest pain, or a new inability to walk. See a physician soon for sudden hearing loss in one ear. Our physician can evaluate new or unexplained dizziness before therapy.

Most Common Cause

BPPV and the Epley Maneuver

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most common cause of vertigo. Tiny calcium crystals in the inner ear drift into one of the balance canals. Then, certain head movements, like rolling over in bed, looking up or bending down, cause brief, intense spinning that usually lasts less than a minute.

A trained clinician confirms BPPV with positional tests that watch for specific eye movements. The most common treatment for the back canal is the Epley maneuver, a series of head and body positions that guides the crystals back where they belong. Other maneuvers treat other canals. The American Academy of Otolaryngology clinical practice guideline recommends repositioning maneuvers as a first-line treatment for BPPV, and many people improve after one or a few sessions. BPPV can return, so your therapist may teach you what to watch for.

Other Causes

Vestibular Neuritis and Other Inner Ear Problems

  • Vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis. Inflammation of the balance nerve or inner ear, often after a virus, causes days of severe vertigo followed by lingering unsteadiness. Vestibular rehab helps the brain adapt.
  • Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD). Ongoing, non-spinning dizziness made worse by busy visual places, like stores. Habituation exercises are a common part of care.
  • Vestibular migraine. Dizziness linked to migraine. Medical care plus vestibular rehab can help; see headache and migraine care.
  • Neck-related dizziness. Some people feel unsteady with neck pain and stiffness. See neck pain physical therapy.
  • Meniere's disease. Episodes of vertigo with hearing changes and ear fullness need care from a physician or ear specialist.

Your Plan

What Vestibular Rehabilitation Involves

Vestibular rehabilitation is exercise-based therapy for dizziness and imbalance. The APTA clinical practice guideline strongly supports it for people with inner ear balance loss. Your plan depends on the cause, and may include:

Gaze stabilization

You keep your eyes on a target while moving your head side to side or up and down. This trains the reflex that keeps vision steady when your head moves.

Habituation

Brief, repeated exposure to the movements or visual settings that trigger mild dizziness helps the brain become less sensitive to them over time.

Balance and walking training

Standing and walking exercises on different surfaces, with head turns and eyes closed, help you feel steady again. Our balance and fall prevention page covers fall risk more broadly.

What to expect

Exercises may make you briefly dizzy, which is part of how they work; your therapist sets a level you can manage. Improvement often comes over several weeks of daily home practice, though timelines vary and can't be promised.

Questions

Vertigo Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Can physical therapy help vertigo?

Often, yes. Physical therapists trained in vestibular rehab treat common causes like BPPV with repositioning maneuvers, and help people with inner ear inflammation or ongoing dizziness through gaze, habituation and balance exercises. Some causes of vertigo need medical care instead, so a clinician first works out what is behind your symptoms before treatment begins.

What is the Epley maneuver?

The Epley maneuver is a sequence of head and body positions that moves loose inner ear crystals out of the back balance canal, the most common type of BPPV. A trained clinician usually does it after confirming the diagnosis with positional testing. Many people improve after one or a few treatments, though BPPV can come back.

Is vertigo a sign of a stroke?

Usually not, but it can be. Dizziness that comes on suddenly with face drooping, arm or leg weakness, slurred speech, double vision or severe loss of balance needs 911 right away. Think BE FAST: balance, eyes, face, arms, speech, time. Brief spinning with head movement is more typical of BPPV.

How long does vestibular therapy take?

It depends on the cause. BPPV often settles after one to a few repositioning sessions. Recovery from vestibular neuritis or longer-lasting dizziness usually takes several weeks of daily home exercise, and sometimes longer. Your therapist tracks your symptoms and balance and adjusts the plan as you progress. Timelines vary and can't be promised.

Can I do the Epley maneuver at home?

Some people are taught a home version once a clinician has confirmed which ear and canal are involved. Doing it on the wrong side or for the wrong canal will not help. Dizziness can also have other causes. Get assessed first, then ask your therapist whether a home maneuver makes sense for you.
