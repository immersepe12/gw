Jaw Pain Basics

What Are TMJ Disorders?

The temporomandibular joints (TMJs) connect your lower jaw to your skull, just in front of each ear. TMJ disorders, also called TMD, are a group of problems affecting these joints and the chewing muscles. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research (NIDCR) notes that TMD is common and that, for many people, symptoms are temporary and improve with simple care.

Common symptoms include:

  • Pain in the jaw, face, around the ear or temples
  • Clicking or popping when you open your mouth, sometimes with pain
  • Limited opening, or a jaw that feels stuck
  • Headaches, and tension in the neck and shoulders
  • Tired jaw muscles after chewing or in the morning

Clenching or grinding, stress, long periods with the head pushed forward, and past jaw injury can all play a part. Clicking without pain often needs no treatment.

Safety First

Jaw Symptoms That Need Medical or Dental Care First

See a physician or dentist promptly, or get emergency care, if you have:

  • Jaw pain with chest pain, shortness of breath or sweating, which can signal a heart attack (call 911)
  • A jaw that locks open or closed and will not move
  • Jaw pain after a blow to the face, or teeth that no longer fit together
  • Fever, facial swelling, or a tooth infection
  • New jaw pain when chewing with scalp tenderness or vision changes, especially over age 50
  • Numbness in the face, or unexplained weight loss

First Steps

Self-Care for TMJ Pain

NIDCR recommends starting with conservative, reversible care. Simple habits that often help:

  • Rest the jaw. Choose softer foods for a while, cut food small, and avoid gum, chewy candy and wide yawns.
  • Use heat or cold. A warm or cool pack on the jaw muscles for 10 to 15 minutes can ease soreness.
  • Find a relaxed jaw position. Lips together, teeth apart, tongue resting lightly on the roof of the mouth.
  • Notice clenching. Many people clench during driving, screen time or stress. Short check-ins during the day help.
  • Manage stress. Relaxation and breathing practice may reduce muscle tension.

NIDCR advises caution about treatments that permanently change your bite or jaw before trying conservative care.

Your Plan

What TMJ Physical Therapy Involves

The evaluation

Your physical therapist checks how wide and how smoothly your mouth opens, jaw muscle tenderness, your neck and posture, and the habits that load your jaw.

Jaw pain exercises

Exercise therapy for TMD has research support. Common examples include:

  • Controlled opening with the tongue on the roof of the mouth, keeping the jaw moving straight
  • Gentle resisted opening and closing using light finger pressure
  • Side-to-side jaw movements within comfort
  • Neck and upper back exercises to ease forward head posture

Posture and neck care

Jaw and neck problems often travel together. Your therapist may include neck mobility and strengthening; see neck pain physical therapy. If headaches are a big part of your symptoms, our physicians can help through headache and migraine care.

Coordinating with your dentist

Dentists often diagnose TMD and may provide a night guard for grinding. We coordinate with your dentist so your therapy and dental care fit together. Improvement often comes over several weeks, though timelines vary and can't be promised.

Questions

TMJ Physical Therapy: Frequently Asked Questions

Can physical therapy help TMJ pain?

Often, yes. Exercise therapy, posture work and hands-on techniques have research support for temporomandibular disorders, and NIDCR recommends starting with conservative care like this. A physical therapist teaches you jaw exercises and habits that reduce strain. We also coordinate with your dentist, who may suggest a night guard if you grind your teeth.

What are good exercises for jaw pain?

Common jaw pain exercises include controlled opening with the tongue resting on the roof of the mouth, gentle resisted opening and closing, and side-to-side movements within comfort. Neck and upper back exercises often help too. Exercises should not cause sharp pain, so a physical therapist can tailor them to your jaw and progress them gradually.

Is jaw clicking a problem?

Not usually by itself. Clicking or popping without pain or locking is common and often needs no treatment. See a dentist or clinician if clicking comes with pain, limited opening, or a jaw that catches or locks. Those signs suggest a joint problem that may benefit from assessment and conservative care.

Should I see a dentist or a physical therapist for TMJ?

Often both. Dentists diagnose TMD, check your teeth and bite, and may provide a night guard. Physical therapists focus on jaw movement, muscle tension, posture and exercise. Working together tends to give a fuller plan. You can start PT without a referral in California, within the direct access limits.

How long does TMJ take to get better?

For many people, TMD symptoms are temporary and settle within weeks to a few months with self-care and conservative treatment. Some people have flare-ups over time, especially during stress. Your physical therapist tracks your jaw opening and pain and adjusts your plan, but recovery timelines vary and can't be promised.
